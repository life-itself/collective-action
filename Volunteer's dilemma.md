# Volunteer's dilemma

A good is provided if at least one person volunteers. Everyone gets the benefit U; the volunteer also pays a cost K, smaller than U. If nobody volunteers, everyone gets nothing. The name and model are Diekmann's (*Journal of Conflict Resolution*, 1985 **[?]**; original not read). The same model and group-size result are in Dixit, Skeath and Reiley (*Games of Strategy*, 4th ed., 2015, pp. 455–457), who base it on Palfrey and Rosenthal (1984) and present it as chicken with mixed strategies.

Two players, U = 4, K = 1, payoffs (row, column):

| | Column volunteers | Column waits |
|---|---|---|
| **Row volunteers** | 3, 3 | 3, 4 |
| **Row waits** | 4, 3 | 0, 0 |

This is [[Chicken (game)]] with a tie: volunteering costs the same whether or not the other also volunteers. Greed, no fear ([[Greed and fear]]).

## Equilibria

- **Pure:** exactly one volunteer. Nobody volunteering is not an equilibrium: if nobody else will, I should.
- **Symmetric mixed:** each waits with probability q = (K/U)^(1/(n−1)). The probability that nobody acts is (K/U)^(n/(n−1)), which rises with n towards K/U. With U = 4 and K = 1, nobody acts 1 time in 16 with two people, and about 1 time in 5 with ten.

Each person is less likely to act in a bigger group, and so is the group as a whole, though the chance of help levels off rather than reaching zero:

> the larger the group, the less likely is help to be given at all.

— Dixit, Skeath and Reiley, *Games of Strategy* (4th ed., 2015), p. 457

This is the formal match to the bystander effect (Darley and Latané 1968 **[?]**).

## Names

Bystander problem, "someone should do something". The course calls it the **volunteer**, or "who gives way". More aliases: [[Names of collective action problems]].

## Typical failure and fix

- **Failure:** waiting, diffusion of responsibility, the wrong person paying. In big groups, nobody acts.
- **Fix:** allocation. Point at one person ("you, call an ambulance"), rotate the duty, take turns, or pay the volunteer. Assurance backfires: if I know you'll act, I wait. A big player who gains enough to act alone often resolves it.

> each prefers the other to do all the work.

— Michael Taylor, *The Possibility of Cooperation* (1987), p. 36, on two cultivators maintaining dykes

Taylor adds that the consequences of nobody doing the work are bad enough that either would do it if the other didn't (p. 36).

## Cases

- A person collapses in a crowded street.
- The Alanya fishing-site rotation, a taking-side case: Ostrom (*Governing the Commons*, 1990, p. 47) says it "cannot be represented as a PD game" and calls it an "assignment" game. The rotation and annual lottery are the fix (pp. 18–20).
- Heartbleed and the under-funded open source maintainer fit here and as a [[Prisoner's Dilemma]]: one big funder is enough, but many small users each gain little.
