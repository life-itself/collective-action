## Question

**What is the simple top-level typology of collective action problems that
actually matters, and what lies beneath it?**

The course uses three patterns now: the battle line (prisoner's dilemma), the
stag hunt (assurance) and the volunteer (chicken). Rufus doesn't find the
volunteer helpful. His challenge, in his words: "aren't many of these things the
same thing? I don't get how a volunteer's dilemma or playing chicken is actually
any different from the prisoner's dilemma."

The test of difference is structural, not a matter of framing. Write each
pattern as a payoff matrix and ask what the equilibria are. In the stag hunt
there are two equilibria; in the prisoner's dilemma there is only one. Patterns
whose matrices have the same equilibrium structure are the same pattern under
different names.

**Pattern**, as used here: a named strategic structure with a typical way of
failing and typical fixes.

## Shape of the answer

**Top level: two axes, kept simple for the audience.**

1. **Equilibrium structure** (from the payoff matrix). Candidate categories, to be tested:
   - One equilibrium, and it's bad: the prisoner's dilemma. An incentive problem.
   - Two equilibria, one better than the other: the stag hunt. A trust problem.
   - Two equilibria where someone has to give way: chicken, and the volunteer's dilemma. A who-pays problem. Test whether this is different enough from the prisoner's dilemma to matter.
   - Two equilibria, which convention to pick: pure coordination, and the battle of the sexes.
2. **Taking or building.** A negative commons that can be overexploited (fish, the atmosphere), or a positive commons that has to be built (information commons, open source, public goods). This is Ostrom's appropriation vs provision.

**Second level: finer axes, useful but not the headline.**

- How many players: two, or many (n-person).
- How contributions add up: sum, best shot (one contribution is enough), weakest link (everyone must contribute), threshold.
- Shape of the return to joining: Schelling's binary-choice curves, Heckathorn's payoff curves, and Marwell and Oliver's critical mass.
- Information, and one-shot vs repeated play.

**Second layer: next to the patterns, not part of them.**

- The principal-agent problem (moral hazard, adverse selection) is what a group faces once it hands a job to someone: "who monitors the monitor" (Alchian and Demsetz).
- Hold-up and lack of commitment are causes.
- Repeated play and the folk theorem, assurance contracts, and the Clarke–Groves and VCG mechanisms are remedies.
- Each item is placed as pattern, cause, remedy, or "adjacent, not a collective action problem".

## Results

Pass 1 draft, 29 Oct. Full working, with payoff matrices, sources and the case
test: [2026-10-29-typology-notes.md](2026-10-29-typology-notes.md). Citation

**Top level: two questions give three patterns.** For any symmetric two-player
game where cooperating together beats defecting together:

- **Greed:** if you pull your weight, do I gain by slacking?
- **Fear:** if I pull my weight alone, do I lose?

| | Fear | No fear |
|---|---|---|
| **Greed** | **Battle line** (prisoner's dilemma). One equilibrium, and it's bad. | **Volunteer, or "who gives way"** (chicken, volunteer's dilemma, battle of the sexes, hold-outs). Someone gives way; the fight is over who. |
| **No greed** | **Stag hunt** (assurance). Two equilibria, one better. | **No problem** (harmony, Olson's privileged group). |

The map covers every symmetric 2×2 game where cooperating together beats
defecting together. The rest, deadlock among them, are not dilemmas.
Heckathorn 1996 (pp. 258–259) derives the same four games by swapping
payoffs in the prisoner's dilemma. Pure coordination (which side of the road)
is a one-line baseline.

**Are chicken and the volunteer the same as the prisoner's dilemma? No.** They
differ by one swap: in chicken, defecting together is the worst outcome, not
cooperating alone. That swap changes:

- **The equilibria.** The prisoner's dilemma has one, where nobody acts. Chicken and the volunteer have equilibria where someone acts, plus a mixed one where disaster has a real chance. In the volunteer's dilemma that chance grows with group size: the bystander effect.
- **The failure.** The wrong person pays, people wait, and there are commitment races.
- **The fix.** Allocate the job: name someone, rotate, pay. Assurance backfires: if I know you'll act, I wait.

Rufus is partly right. The outcome looks the same (nothing gets done), real
cases slide between patterns as the payoffs change, and repeated play blurs the
line.

**Second axis: taking or building**, defined as restraint vs contribution. It
rests on Ostrom's split between taking from a common resource and providing it
(*Governing the Commons*, pp. 30–31, 47–50), simplified. In a simple matrix it
doesn't change the equilibria, but the remedies differ:

- Taking needs limits, boundaries and monitoring.
- Building needs selective incentives, assurance and patrons.

Information commons are mostly building, with a taking side (enclosure,
pollution).

**Second layer.**

- Principal-agent problems are adjacent: they involve two parties, not a group. They become a cause once a group delegates monitoring or enforcement ("who monitors the monitor").
- Moral hazard in teams and the second-order free-rider problem are battle lines one level down.
- Hold-up and lack of commitment are causes.
- Repetition, selective incentives, assurance contracts, Clarke–Groves and VCG, rotation and lotteries are remedies.

**Case test.**

- **No pattern fits:** Twelve Angry Men (an information cascade); the Aral Sea, the Ik, the Donner Party and *Jaws* (not collective action structures). Meerkats, and probably leaded petrol, aren't dilemmas at all ("no problem").
- **Fit two patterns:** about 13, including hoplites, the playground, Heartbleed, climate, the naval race, and the AI race (which fits all three). Almost all are many-player threshold cases, or cases whose payoffs shift with a parameter. That is a teaching point ("the pattern is in the payoffs, and payoffs move"), not a reason to add patterns.
- **Chapter 1 grid:** it marks taking × volunteer "(rare)", which is wrong. The Alanya fishing-site rotation (Ostrom p. 47) and Taylor's threshold fishery belong there.
