## Summary

In *Goliath's Curse* (2025), Luke Kemp makes a short argument that conflict is not purely destructive. External threat raises cooperation inside a group and raises hostility toward outsiders. He rests the first claim on the review in [[Bauer et al on war and cooperation]] (sixteen studies, forty countries), and adds three supports:

- **Symbols and stories.** Attacked groups develop shared symbols and behaviors, such as patriotism. His example is US flag sales after 9/11: Walmart sold 250,000 flags the next day, against 10,000 on the same day a year earlier.
- **Tightness and prejudice.** Across societies, exposure to threats such as war, disease and scarcity is the best predictor of prejudice and 'cultural tightness' (stricter norms, harsher punishments, less tolerance of deviance).
- **Neural and organizational evidence.** In one study, the brainwaves of Chinese students synchronized while they read about a threat from Japan, and they coordinated better on group tasks. Kemp links this to the bonding seen in terrorist cells, revolutionary groups and military units, and to military training that rebuilds recruits into a single team.

The claim in the last line of the passage is that the same mechanism raises both in-group cooperation and the capacity for violence against outsiders. This is the parochial pattern Bauer et al flag as preliminary.

## Excerpts

> Conflict isn't a completely destructive force. It can spur cooperation, at least within a group.

> A people under threat stand together but apart from others.

> An external threat supercharges cooperation within groups and the capacity for violence against outsiders.

— Luke Kemp, *Goliath's Curse: The History and Future of Societal Collapse* (2025), on conflict and cooperation; the passage cites Bauer et al in its endnote 47 (page number not recorded)

## So what

The same threat that makes a group cooperate also defines who is outside it. For coordination problems that need a large or global in-group, such as those the metacrisis literature describes, threat-driven bonding is an unreliable tool, since it works by drawing a boundary. The open design question is whether the cooperative effect can be had without the 'other', for example with a shared threat that no group is cast as the source of.
