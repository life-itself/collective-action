# Contemporary examples — candidate list

Candidates, not selections. Range is roughly 1980 to the present, chosen to connect
the theory to the domains course participants work in. Successes and failures both,
because a list of failures teaches only that the problem is hard.

Each entry runs the five diagnostic questions from [arc.md](../arc.md) explicitly,
plus a sixth line on substitutes — whether a cheap technical alternative to the
harmful behavior existed. That sixth question is the standard explanation for why
Montreal worked and Kyoto didn't, and it is doing more work in these cases than
any of the other five.

Figures here have been checked against the sources cited. Where a number is
contested or a judgment is early, that's said in the entry.

## Climate and atmosphere

### The Montreal Protocol

- **What happened:** Farman, Gardiner and Shanklin published the Antarctic ozone
  hole in *Nature* in May 1985. The Vienna Convention was agreed in March 1985 and
  the Montreal Protocol signed in September 1987, entering force January 1989.
  It now has 198 parties — universal ratification, the only UN treaty to reach it —
  and has phased out over 99% of production and consumption of controlled
  ozone-depleting substances. WMO's 2022 assessment projects a return to 1980
  ozone values around 2040 globally, 2045 over the Arctic and 2066 over the
  Antarctic.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A global commons with a classic free-rider
  structure — each country's CFC emissions damage everyone's ozone layer, and
  unilateral abstention buys nothing. Solved, and comprehensively.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* Effectively a few dozen producing countries, and a very
    small number of chemical firms — DuPont, ICI, Atochem, Allied Signal. Not 198.
  - *Can you see defection?* Yes. Production is industrial and traceable, and
    atmospheric monitoring detects emissions independently of self-reporting.
  - *Can you punish it?* Yes. Article 4 bans trade in controlled substances with
    non-parties, which converts the treaty into a club with a membership benefit.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes — annual Meetings of the Parties, with a standing
    scientific and technology assessment process that has amended the protocol
    repeatedly (London 1990, Copenhagen 1992, Montreal 1997, Beijing 1999,
    Kigali 2016).
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* Close. The US had already banned CFC
    aerosols in 1978, and DuPont — holding roughly a quarter of the world market —
    announced a total phase-out in March 1988, which reset what the industry
    treated as feasible.
  - *Substitutes?* Yes, and this is the core of it. HCFCs and HFCs were available
    and cheap, and the firms that would be regulated held the patents on the
    replacements. Compliance cost was low and the incumbents stood to profit.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Did it ever work?* (the anchor case). Session 1,
  *Introduction to collective action*, as the opening success. Session 4,
  *Changing the payoffs*, on the trade-ban mechanism.
- **Contested:** The standard framing — "the treaty that proves it can be done" —
  is disputed by the people who study it most closely. Barrett's argument in
  *Environment and Statecraft* is that Montreal is an easy case: small compliance
  cost, concentrated industry, available substitutes, a trade restriction that
  actually bit. Sunstein's comparison adds that the US ran a domestic
  cost-benefit analysis showing large net domestic benefits from unilateral
  action — the reverse of the climate arithmetic. Teaching Montreal as a template
  is the mistake; teaching it as a diagnostic result is the point.
- **Media:** Good data visualizations exist — NASA Ozone Watch publishes the
  Antarctic ozone hole area time series annually, which is the single best
  "it worked" chart in the course. Primary sources: the protocol text and all
  amendments at ozone.unep.org. No major documentary known.
- **Source:** Farman, Gardiner & Shanklin, "Large losses of total ozone in
  Antarctica reveal seasonal ClO<sub>x</sub>/NO<sub>x</sub> interaction",
  *Nature* 315, 1985. Ozone Secretariat, "Facts and figures on ozone protection"
  (ozone.unep.org/facts-and-figures-ozone-protection). WMO/UNEP *Scientific
  Assessment of Ozone Depletion: 2022*. Scott Barrett, *Environment and
  Statecraft*, OUP 2003. Cass Sunstein, "Of Montreal and Kyoto: A Tale of Two
  Protocols", *Harvard Environmental Law Review* 31, 2007.

### The CFC-11 defection, 2013–2019

- **What happened:** Montzka et al. reported in *Nature* in 2018 that global
  CFC-11 emissions had stopped declining and risen from around 2013, in breach of
  the protocol. Rigby et al. (2019) traced roughly half the global increase to
  eastern China using atmospheric measurements from Gosan, South Korea and
  Hateruma, Japan. Two *Nature* papers in February 2021 reported that emissions
  had fallen back to pre-2013 levels by 2019 — eastern China down by
  10 ± 3 Gg/yr from 2014–2017 levels.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The enforcement half of Montreal, tested
  live. A treaty with no inspectorate detected a violation from the atmosphere and
  the violation stopped within about two years.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* A handful of illicit foam-blowing producers, within one
    country.
  - *Can you see defection?* This is the whole point of the case. Yes — not
    through reporting, which showed nothing, but through a global network of
    background air monitoring stations. Detection was physical, not
    administrative.
  - *Can you punish it?* Domestically, yes: Chinese authorities acted after the
    attribution was published. Internationally, reputational cost plus the
    existing trade sanctions.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes, continuously.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* Not relevant here; the question is
    monitoring, not provision.
  - *Substitutes?* Yes — legal blowing agents existed; CFC-11 was used because it
    was cheaper.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Did it ever work?*, as the follow-up beat to
  Montreal. Session 3, *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem*, as a concrete
  instance of "can you see defection" being an engineering question with an
  answer.
- **Media:** The *Nature* papers carry publication-quality emissions charts. The
  Conversation piece by Rigby and Park ("How we traced 'mystery emissions' of CFCs
  back to eastern China", 2019) is a readable first-person account of the
  detection.
- **Source:** Montzka et al., "An unexpected and persistent increase in global
  emissions of ozone-depleting CFC-11", *Nature* 557, 2018. Rigby et al.,
  "Increase in CFC-11 emissions from eastern China based on atmospheric
  observations", *Nature* 569, 2019. Park et al., "A decline in emissions of
  CFC-11 and related chemicals from eastern China", *Nature* 590, 2021.
  Montzka et al., "A decline in global CFC-11 emissions during 2018−2019",
  *Nature* 590, 2021.

### The Kigali Amendment

- **What happened:** HFCs, the replacement for CFCs, turned out to be potent
  greenhouse gases. The Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol, agreed October
  2016 and in force from January 2019, phases them down; there were 172 parties as
  of December 2025. NOAA's estimate is that compliance avoids 0.3–0.5°C of warming
  by 2100 relative to no controls.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A climate agreement smuggled into an
  ozone treaty, and by some distance the most effective one. It works because it
  inherits Montreal's machinery — the trade provisions, the assessment panels, the
  funding mechanism.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* Same concentrated refrigerant and air-conditioning
    industry.
  - *Can you see defection?* Yes, by the same atmospheric route.
  - *Can you punish it?* Yes, inherited trade restrictions.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes, the same annual cycle.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* No, but the US ratified in 2022 with
    unusual bipartisan support, driven by domestic manufacturers who wanted the
    global standard set where their products already were.
  - *Substitutes?* Yes — HFOs and natural refrigerants. Same structure as
    Montreal, a third time.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Did it ever work?*, as the counter-example to
  "climate is just harder": a climate agreement that worked, because it was
  shaped like an ozone agreement.
- **Media:** None known beyond UNEP explainers.
- **Source:** Ozone Secretariat, "Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol"
  (ozone.unep.org/kigali-amendment-overview). Congressional Research Service,
  "Hydrofluorocarbon Phasedown: Background and Issues Facing Congress", IF11779.

### The Kyoto Protocol

- **What happened:** Adopted December 1997, setting binding targets for 37
  industrialized countries and the EU — an average 5% cut below 1990 levels over
  2008–2012. The US signed but never ratified; Bush formally repudiated it in
  2001, with the US then accounting for roughly a third of Annex I emissions.
  Canada withdrew in December 2011, citing the absence of the US and China and a
  potential $14bn liability. Developing countries, including China, had no
  targets. The treaty entered force only in February 2005, after Russia ratified.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A global public good where the largest
  emitters faced the largest costs and the benefits accrued to everyone
  regardless. Failed as a global instrument. The EU did meet its first-period
  target, which matters for how the failure is described.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* Nominally 37 Annex B parties; in substance, every
    economy on earth, because emissions are not concentrated in an industry the
    way CFC production was.
  - *Can you see defection?* Partly. National emissions inventories are
    self-reported and were, at the time, not independently verifiable at treaty
    precision. Contrast Montreal, where the atmosphere told on you.
  - *Can you punish it?* No. The compliance mechanism's ultimate sanction was
    stricter targets in a future period you could simply leave — which Canada
    did, at no cost.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes, but the shadow of the future is weak when the
    penalty for defection is a future obligation.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* No. No country's unilateral abatement
    materially changes its own climate outcome. This is the structural difference
    from the US position on ozone.
  - *Substitutes?* No — not in 1997. Fossil energy had no cheap drop-in
    replacement, abatement cost fell on the whole economy rather than a few
    chemical firms, and no incumbent stood to profit from the switch. This is the
    single largest difference from Montreal.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Did it ever work?* — the other half of the named
  contrast pair. Session 2, *The games*, as a clean public-goods free-rider
  structure.
- **Contested:** "Kyoto failed" is the shorthand and it flattens things. EU
  emissions in 2020 were 31% below 1990 levels, well past the 8% target, and the
  protocol built the emissions-accounting and market infrastructure that Paris
  later used. The honest version: it failed at the thing it was for, which was
  binding the large emitters.
- **Media:** UNFCCC holds the primary documents and the full ratification record.
  Our World in Data has good per-country emissions series for constructing the
  before/after. No strong documentary known.
- **Source:** UNFCCC, "Kyoto Protocol — Targets for the first commitment period".
  Al Jazeera, "Canada quits Kyoto climate protocol", 13 December 2011.
  Sunstein, "Of Montreal and Kyoto", 2007.

### The Paris Agreement

- **What happened:** Adopted 12 December 2015, in force 4 November 2016.
  Abandoned binding targets for nationally determined contributions plus a
  ratchet-and-review cycle. Climate Action Tracker's November 2025 update puts
  warming under current policies at 2.6°C, unchanged for four years; the pledges
  and targets scenario worsened from 2.1°C to 2.2°C, largely because the US
  withdrawal invalidated its targets. CAT also notes the current-policies
  projection has fallen roughly 1°C from the 3.6°C estimated in 2015.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A deliberate architectural bet — that
  transparency, peer pressure and repeated review can substitute for enforcement.
  The result is neither the success nor the failure it gets called.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* ~195 parties, all of them.
  - *Can you see defection?* Better than under Kyoto. The Enhanced Transparency
    Framework plus satellite and atmospheric monitoring have made emissions much
    harder to misreport, and independent trackers publish the gap.
  - *Can you punish it?* No. There is no sanction, by design. The US has now left
    twice.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes, on a five-year ratchet.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* No, though China's solar and battery
    manufacturing has done more to change the cost curve than any treaty
    provision.
  - *Substitutes?* Increasingly, yes — and this is the interesting part. Solar,
    wind and batteries became cheap between 2015 and now, which moves climate
    slowly toward the Montreal structure. The 1°C improvement in the
    current-policies projection is substitutes arriving, not enforcement working.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Did it ever work?* Session 4, *Polycentricity* —
  Paris is close to Ostrom's answer applied at planetary scale, and worth
  examining as such rather than as a weak treaty. Session 6, *Why the current
  stack can't hold*.
- **Contested:** Judging Paris is premature and people on both sides do it anyway.
  It has not delivered 1.5°C and will not. It has coincided with a 1°C improvement
  in the projected outcome. Whether the agreement caused any of that, or whether
  it is technology cost curves that would have moved regardless, is genuinely
  unsettled.
- **Media:** Climate Action Tracker's thermometer graphic is the standard
  visualization and is updated annually. UNEP *Emissions Gap Report* each year.
  Primary text at unfccc.int.
- **Source:** Climate Action Tracker, "Little change in warming outlook for four
  years; new 2035 climate targets make no difference", Global Update,
  13 November 2025. UNEP, *Emissions Gap Report 2025*.

### The US Acid Rain Program

- **What happened:** Title IV of the 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments created a
  cap-and-trade system for power sector SO<sub>2</sub>, aiming at a permanent
  10 million ton cut from 1980 levels, with the 2010 cap set at 8.95 million tons
  — about half of 1980 power sector emissions. By 2002, SO<sub>2</sub> from
  affected sources was 10.2 million tons, down 35% from 1990. Costs came in well
  below the original projections.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A commons problem — acid deposition —
  solved within one jurisdiction by a coercive cap plus a market in the residual.
  Useful precisely because it's the sub-global case: the same structure, with a
  sovereign present.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* A few hundred generating units, all within one legal
    system.
  - *Can you see defection?* Yes, and this is the underrated part: the program
    mandated continuous emissions monitoring on every unit. Observation was
    engineered before the market was built.
  - *Can you punish it?* Yes — automatic penalties per excess ton, no discretion.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes, indefinitely.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* No, which is why the sovereign did it.
  - *Substitutes?* Yes — low-sulfur Powder River Basin coal, and scrubbers, both
    of which got cheaper than expected once rail deregulation cut transport costs.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Coercion*, as Hardin's "mutual coercion mutually
  agreed upon" in its cleanest modern form. Session 4, *Changing the payoffs*.
- **Media:** EPA publishes long emissions and deposition time series. Good
  before/after deposition maps exist.
- **Source:** US EPA, "1990 Clean Air Act Amendment Summary: Title IV" and "Acid
  Rain Program". Chestnut & Mills, "A fresh look at the benefits and costs of the
  US acid rain program", *Journal of Environmental Management* 77, 2005.

### The global leaded petrol phase-out

- **What happened:** UNEP announced on 30 August 2021 that the era of leaded
  petrol was over, after service stations in Algeria — the last country using it —
  stopped supplying it in July 2021. The UNEP-led Partnership for Clean Fuels and
  Vehicles ran the campaign for roughly two decades; sub-Saharan Africa went
  unleaded in 2006. UNEP's estimate is over 1.2 million premature deaths avoided
  annually.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A cross-border pollution problem with a
  race-to-the-bottom in fuel standards, resolved country by country rather than by
  treaty. Interesting because there was no treaty.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* Nominally every country, but in practice a small number
    of refiners and a single dominant additive producer.
  - *Can you see defection?* Yes — fuel at the pump is testable, and stocks are
    traceable.
  - *Can you punish it?* Weakly. Mostly aid conditionality, technical assistance
    and vehicle import standards rather than sanction.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* The US and EU bans effectively set the
    direction, because catalytic converters are destroyed by leaded fuel and the
    global vehicle fleet is a hand-me-down from rich countries.
  - *Substitutes?* Yes. Unleaded formulations were straightforward and the
    refinery retrofits were affordable, which is why the campaign could proceed
    without any enforcement mechanism at all.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Did it ever work?* — the third data point after
  Montreal and Kyoto, and the one that shows the mechanism was substitutes rather
  than treaty design. Session 1, *Introduction to collective action*.
- **Media:** UNEP press release and UNifeed video from 30 August 2021. Blood lead
  level time series make strong charts.
- **Source:** UNEP, "Era of leaded petrol over, eliminating a major threat to
  human and planetary health", 30 August 2021.

## AI

### The frontier AI race

- **What happened:** From roughly 2020, a small number of labs — OpenAI,
  Google DeepMind, Anthropic, Meta, xAI, and Chinese labs including DeepSeek and
  Zhipu — have scaled models under competitive pressure, with training compute and
  capital expenditure rising steeply year on year. Several labs were founded on
  explicit safety rationales and each has publicly argued that unilateral slowing
  would hand the lead to a less careful competitor.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** An arms race in Schelling's sense. The
  distinctive feature is that participants state the trap openly in their own
  founding documents, which makes it the cleanest available case of structure
  overriding intention.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* Small — under ten at the frontier, which by Olson's logic
    should make agreement possible. It has not, so far.
  - *Can you see defection?* Partly. Training runs of frontier scale are visible
    through hardware procurement and data center energy draw; internal safety
    practice is not visible at all. Capability is observable, care is not.
  - *Can you punish it?* Not really. Export controls act on states rather than
    labs; reputational cost is real but small relative to the returns.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes, constantly — the labs share staff, conferences
    and investors. High interaction has not produced cooperation, which is worth
    sitting with.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* No. Nvidia and TSMC are closer to being
    chokepoints than any lab.
  - *Substitutes?* No. There is no cheaper way to get the capability, and no
    technical alternative that delivers the payoff without the risk. This is the
    Kyoto position, not the Montreal one.
- **Could serve:** Session 1, *The pattern beneath the metacrisis* and *Why this
  matters so much now*. Session 4, *Schelling* — the highest-value pairing in the
  course, since the nuclear literature was written for exactly this shape.
  Session 2, *The many names* (multipolar trap).
- **Media:** Epoch AI publishes the training compute dataset and charts, which are
  the best available quantitative visualization. Primary sources: the founding
  charters and safety frameworks of the labs, which state the dilemma explicitly.
- **Source:** Epoch AI, Notable AI Models database (epoch.ai). Thomas Schelling,
  *The Strategy of Conflict*, 1960, for the structure.

### AI summits and voluntary commitments

- **What happened:** The UK convened the first AI Safety Summit at Bletchley Park
  on 1–2 November 2023, producing the Bletchley Declaration. At the Seoul summit
  of 21–22 May 2024, sixteen companies — including Amazon, Anthropic, Google, IBM,
  Microsoft, OpenAI, Cohere, Mistral, G42, Naver, Samsung and Zhipu — signed the
  Frontier AI Safety Commitments, agreeing to publish safety frameworks defining
  intolerable risk thresholds. By the Paris AI Action Summit of 10–11 February
  2025, the framing had shifted from safety to action and investment; 61 countries
  and organizations signed the closing statement and the US and UK both declined.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** An attempt to build the cooperation
  infrastructure — common standards, mutual observation, a repeated meeting — in
  advance of any enforcement. The two-year trajectory from Bletchley to Paris is
  the case, more than any single summit.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* Sixteen firms and around thirty governments at Seoul.
    Small enough, on paper.
  - *Can you see defection?* Only through self-published frameworks. There is no
    audit. Third-party trackers like AI Lab Watch exist and depend on voluntary
    disclosure.
  - *Can you punish it?* No. The commitments are voluntary and carry no penalty.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes — the summit series continues, with India hosting
    in 2026.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* The US is, and its February 2025 refusal
    to sign demonstrated that a single large player leaving is enough to change
    what the agreement means.
  - *Substitutes?* No.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Did it ever work?* — as the live case rather than
  the historical one. Session 3, *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem*, on
  what repetition alone can and can't deliver without observation or punishment.
- **Media:** Full summit texts and signatory lists are public
  (gov.uk for Bletchley and Seoul; the Paris statement via the French government).
  Future of Life Institute maintains a summit archive and an AI Safety Index
  scoring lab frameworks.
- **Source:** UK Government, "Frontier AI Safety Commitments, AI Seoul Summit
  2024". CNBC, "U.S. and Britain snub international AI accord in Paris",
  11 February 2025. Future of Life Institute, AI Safety Summits project page.

### The EU AI Act, and the regulatory-race question

- **What happened:** The EU AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024, with
  prohibitions applying from February 2025 and general-purpose AI model
  obligations from 2 August 2025. In 2026 the Digital Omnibus package pushed back
  parts of the timetable, with full applicability of the remaining provisions now
  scheduled for 2 August 2028.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A test of whether one jurisdiction can
  set a global floor — the Brussels effect — against a race to the bottom in which
  each jurisdiction has an incentive to be the lax one and capture the investment.
  The 2026 delay is evidence about which way it's going.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* Three regulatory blocs that matter, plus every firm.
  - *Can you see defection?* By jurisdictions, yes — legislation is public. By
    firms, partly, through conformity assessment.
  - *Can you punish it?* Within the EU, yes: fines up to 7% of global turnover.
    Between jurisdictions, no.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* This is exactly the claim being tested.
    The EU is a large enough market that compliance often propagates globally, as
    it did with GDPR. Whether that holds for AI is open.
  - *Substitutes?* Not applicable in the usual sense; the relevant question is
    whether compliant models are competitive.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Coercion*. Session 4, *Polycentricity* — a
  stacked rather than global solution. Session 6, *Why the current stack can't
  hold*.
- **Media:** artificialintelligenceact.eu maintains an annotated text and
  implementation timeline. Primary text in the Official Journal.
- **Source:** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689. EU AI Act implementation timeline,
  artificialintelligenceact.eu. DLA Piper, "Latest wave of obligations under the
  EU AI Act take effect", August 2025.

### California SB 1047 and SB 53

- **What happened:** SB 1047 would have imposed safety testing and shutdown
  requirements on frontier model developers, plus whistleblower protections.
  It passed both chambers and Governor Newsom vetoed it on 29 September 2024,
  citing the lack of empirical grounding and the breadth of its standards. Exactly
  a year later, on 29 September 2025, he signed SB 53, the Transparency in
  Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act — narrower, disclosure-focused, and the
  first US frontier AI law.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A sub-national jurisdiction attempting to
  regulate an industry that can relocate, under explicit pressure that regulation
  would push developers out of the state. The veto and the later signature show
  the bargaining range.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* A handful of developers; fifty states; one industry
    concentrated in one of them.
  - *Can you see defection?* Under SB 53, partly — disclosure is mandated but
    unverified.
  - *Can you punish it?* Within California, yes, modestly. A developer can also
    incorporate elsewhere.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes — annual legislative sessions, which is why the
    second attempt succeeded.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* California hosts most frontier labs, so
    it is the closest thing to a unilateral actor in US AI policy.
  - *Substitutes?* Not applicable.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Did it ever work?* Session 4, *Polycentricity*, as
  a federated attempt at a problem no single state owns.
- **Media:** Bill texts and the veto message are public on leginfo.ca.gov. Good
  contemporaneous coverage from CalMatters and NPR.
- **Source:** CalMatters, "Newsom vetoes major California artificial intelligence
  bill", September 2024. Future of Privacy Forum, "California's SB 53: The First
  Frontier AI Law, Explained", 2025.

## Weapons

### The Non-Proliferation Treaty

- **What happened:** Opened for signature 1968, in force 1970, extended
  indefinitely in 1995. It now has 191 states parties, the widest adherence of any
  arms control treaty. India, Israel, Pakistan and South Sudan never joined;
  North Korea announced withdrawal in 2003. Kennedy's 1963 estimate of 15–25
  nuclear-armed states by the 1970s did not happen: there are nine. South Africa
  dismantled its arsenal; Ukraine, Belarus and Kazakhstan transferred theirs to
  Russia; Argentina, Brazil, Sweden, Taiwan and South Korea abandoned programs.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The best available evidence that a
  large-N security dilemma can be held for fifty years — and simultaneously the
  best evidence about what that costs, since the bargain has always been that five
  states keep theirs.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* 191 parties, but the binding constraint is the ~40 states
    with the technical capacity to build one.
  - *Can you see defection?* Yes, increasingly. IAEA safeguards, the Additional
    Protocol, seismic and radionuclide monitoring under the CTBTO, and commercial
    satellite imagery. Enrichment is hard to hide, though Iraq, Libya, Iran,
    Syria and North Korea all got further than safeguards caught.
  - *Can you punish it?* Yes — sanctions, and in several cases military action.
    The punishment is real, which distinguishes this from the BWC.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes — five-yearly Review Conferences, the next in 2026.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* Yes, and that's the mechanism. US
    security guarantees are what made non-acquisition rational for Germany, Japan
    and South Korea. Extended deterrence is a selective incentive in Olson's
    sense.
  - *Substitutes?* Yes, of a kind — alliance protection substitutes for an
    arsenal. Where the substitute is credible, states don't build. Where it
    erodes, this case reopens.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Did it ever work?* Session 4, *Schelling*.
  Session 1, *War*. Session 5, *The bill* — the treaty is explicitly a two-tier
  bargain, an in-group that keeps what the out-group is denied.
- **Contested:** Whether the NPT caused the non-proliferation, or whether alliance
  guarantees and the sheer cost of programs did the work and the treaty
  ratified it, is a live argument.
- **Media:** Arms Control Association fact sheets are the standard reference and
  are kept current. Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists' Nuclear Notebook gives
  warhead-count time series — a strong visualization, since global stockpiles
  peaked around 70,000 in 1986 and are now around 12,000.
- **Source:** Arms Control Association, "The Nuclear Nonproliferation Treaty (NPT)
  at a Glance". UNODA treaty page. IAEA, "Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of
  Nuclear Weapons".

### The expiry of New START

- **What happened:** New START, signed 2010, capped deployed US and Russian
  strategic warheads at 1,550 each and provided for on-site inspection. Russia
  suspended participation in 2023. Putin proposed on 22 September 2025 that both
  sides continue observing the central limits informally for a year past expiry.
  The treaty expired on 5 February 2026 with no formal US response, leaving the
  two largest arsenals under no legally binding limits for the first time since
  1972.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A two-player repeated game with explicit
  verification, which has now lapsed. It's the cleanest small-N case in the
  course, and it just failed in real time.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* Two. Olson says this should be the easy case.
  - *Can you see defection?* Under the treaty, yes — 18 on-site inspections a
    year plus data exchanges plus national technical means. Without it, satellite
    imagery only, which counts launchers rather than warheads.
  - *Can you punish it?* Only by matching — build-up for build-up.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes, indefinitely, which is why arms control worked at
    all through the Cold War.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* Either party can set the level
    unilaterally, and each has.
  - *Substitutes?* No.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Schelling*, as the live example. Session 1, *War*.
  Session 3, *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem* — verification regimes
  are folk-theorem engineering, and the case shows what happens when you remove
  the observation channel from a game that otherwise has every advantage.
- **Media:** Arms Control Association *Arms Control Today* covered the expiry
  (March 2026 issue). Federation of American Scientists publishes deployed-warhead
  time series.
- **Source:** Arms Control Association, "New START Expires As U.S. Urges
  'Modernized' Treaty", *Arms Control Today*, March 2026. Congressional Research
  Service, "Extension of New START Central Limits", IN12640.

### The Biological Weapons Convention and the failed verification protocol

- **What happened:** The BWC opened for signature in 1972 and entered force
  26 March 1975; it has 187 states parties. It bans development and stockpiling
  and contains no verification machinery. From 1994 an Ad Hoc Group negotiated a
  protocol requiring facility declarations, random visits and challenge
  investigations. In July 2001 the US rejected the draft and withdrew from the
  negotiation; it has never been revived. The Implementation Support Unit, the
  treaty's entire permanent staff, is three people in Geneva.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** An agreement with near-universal
  membership, no observation and no punishment. It's the control condition for the
  diagnostic: what a treaty looks like when only questions 1 and 4 get a yes.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* 187 parties, and a growing number of non-state actors with
    the relevant capability — which is the direction of travel that makes this
    worse over time.
  - *Can you see defection?* No. Dual-use equipment is identical to legitimate
    pharmaceutical and vaccine plant. The Soviet Biopreparat program employed tens
    of thousands while the USSR was a party, and was revealed by defectors rather
    than detected.
  - *Can you punish it?* Formally, complaint to the Security Council, subject to
    veto. The mechanism has never been used.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes — Review Conferences every five years, which have
    repeatedly failed to agree anything substantive.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* No.
  - *Substitutes?* Not applicable — the relevant asymmetry is that the offensive
    and defensive research are the same research.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Did it ever work?* — as the failure whose cause is
  unusually legible. Session 1, *The pattern beneath the metacrisis*.
  Session 1, *Why this matters so much now*, on falling capability barriers.
- **Media:** Ken Alibek's *Biohazard* (1999) is the insider account of Biopreparat.
  Primary documents at the UN Office for Disarmament Affairs. No good
  visualizations known.
- **Source:** Arms Control Association, "The Biological Weapons Convention (BWC)
  At A Glance". Marie Chevrier, "The Biological Weapons Convention: the protocol
  that almost was", *Verification Yearbook 2001*. US GAO, "Arms Control: Efforts
  to Strengthen the Biological Weapons Convention", GAO-02-1038NI.

### Gain-of-function research and the self-imposed moratorium

- **What happened:** After the 2011 Fouchier and Kawaoka H5N1 transmissibility
  experiments, researchers declared a voluntary 60-day moratorium in January 2012,
  extended to about a year. On 17 October 2014 the US government imposed a funding
  pause on gain-of-function work on influenza, MERS and SARS viruses, affecting 18
  projects, 7 of which were later exempted. The pause was lifted in December 2017
  and replaced by the HHS P3CO review framework. A revised OSTP policy was issued
  in May 2024 with an effective date of 6 May 2025.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A field trying to restrain itself, with
  the restraint applying only to one country's funding and not to the research
  anywhere else. The 1975 Asilomar conference on recombinant DNA is the usual
  precedent and the usual reason people think this can work.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* A few hundred labs worldwide with the capability, in
    dozens of jurisdictions. Rising.
  - *Can you see defection?* Weakly. Publication reveals work after it's done;
    unpublished work is invisible. Biosafety inspections are national and
    inconsistent.
  - *Can you punish it?* Only through funding, and only within the funding
    jurisdiction. A researcher who loses NIH support can work elsewhere.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes, within a small and reputationally dense scientific
    community — which is the strongest thing this case has going for it, and is
    what Asilomar ran on.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* The NIH is the largest single funder, so
    US policy moves a large fraction of the world's work. Not all of it.
  - *Substitutes?* Partly. Much of the scientific goal is reachable through
    safer methods — pseudoviruses, structural prediction, surveillance — which is
    the main argument that restraint is affordable here.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Did it ever work?* Session 3, *Repeated interaction
  and the folk theorem*, on reputation in a small community as an enforcement
  mechanism. Session 1, *Why this matters so much now*.
- **Media:** Primary sources: the 2014 White House announcement, NIH notice
  NOT-OD-17-071, the 2024 OSTP policy. Congressional Research Service reports
  IF12021 and R47114 are good neutral summaries of a politically hot topic.
- **Source:** NIAID, "NIH Removes Gain-of-Function Research Pause". Congressional
  Research Service, "Oversight of Gain-of-Function Research with Pathogens",
  R47114. Paul Berg et al. on Asilomar, *PNAS* 72, 1975.

## Health

### Antimicrobial resistance

- **What happened:** The GRAM Project's 2022 *Lancet* analysis estimated 1.27
  million deaths in 2019 directly attributable to bacterial antimicrobial
  resistance, with AMR associated with 4.95 million deaths — more than HIV/AIDS or
  malaria. A 2024 GRAM forecast projects over 39 million cumulative deaths
  directly attributable between 2025 and 2050. Meanwhile the antibiotic
  development pipeline has largely emptied: most large pharmaceutical firms
  exited antibacterial R&D, and several small developers that brought new
  antibiotics to market went bankrupt doing it.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Two problems stacked. Overuse is a
  subtractable commons — every prescription and every kilogram fed to livestock
  depletes a shared resource. Development is a positive commons — a new antibiotic
  that should be held in reserve earns nothing, so nobody makes one. The good
  stewardship that preserves the resource destroys the market that replenishes it.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* Effectively unbounded: every prescriber, every farmer,
    every patient, in every country.
  - *Can you see defection?* Poorly. Consumption data is patchy, over-the-counter
    sale without prescription is common in much of the world, and agricultural use
    is often unreported.
  - *Can you punish it?* Barely. The EU banned antibiotic growth promoters in
    2006 and requires veterinary prescription; enforcement elsewhere ranges from
    partial to absent.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes, but the actors are anonymous to each other. This
    is the failure mode Ostrom's design principles are built to avoid, at a scale
    where none of them apply.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* No. Resistance genes cross borders on
    aircraft and in food.
  - *Substitutes?* This is the crux. For the overuse problem, often yes — many
    prescriptions are unnecessary. For the replacement problem, no: there is no
    substitute for a working antibiotic, and the pull-incentive schemes (the UK
    NHS subscription model, the proposed US PASTEUR Act) exist precisely because
    the market cannot supply one.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Ostrom and the commons* — it exhibits both the
  positive and the subtractable commons in one case, which is the distinction the
  module is built on. Session 1, *The pattern beneath the metacrisis*.
  Session 4, *Changing the payoffs*, on pull incentives.
- **Media:** The GRAM Project publishes an interactive country-level visualization
  tool (vizhub.healthdata.org/microbe). The 2016 O'Neill Review for the UK
  government is the standard policy document and has the widely reproduced
  "10 million deaths by 2050" figure — which is a projection, frequently quoted as
  though it were a measurement.
- **Source:** GBD 2019 Antimicrobial Resistance Collaborators, "Global burden of
  bacterial antimicrobial resistance in 2019: a systematic analysis", *The Lancet*
  399, 2022. GRAM Project, "Global burden of bacterial antimicrobial resistance
  1990–2021 and forecasts to 2050", *The Lancet*, 2024. Jim O'Neill, *Tackling
  Drug-Resistant Infections Globally*, 2016.

### COVAX and vaccine nationalism

- **What happened:** COVAX launched in June 2020 to pool procurement and
  distribute COVID-19 vaccines equitably. It aimed at 2 billion doses in 2021,
  cut the target to 1.425 billion by September 2021, and missed that too. High
  income countries bought up early supply bilaterally; India's export restrictions
  on the Serum Institute removed the largest planned supplier at the critical
  moment. COVAX closed on 31 December 2023 having delivered nearly 2 billion doses
  to 146 economies — the right total, two years late.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Every government's dominant strategy was
  to secure doses for its own population first, and the collectively optimal
  allocation — vaccinating the vulnerable everywhere before the young anywhere —
  was available to nobody unilaterally.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* ~190 governments plus a handful of manufacturers.
  - *Can you see defection?* Yes, immediately and publicly. Advance purchase
    agreements and export bans were reported in real time. Transparency turned out
    not to help.
  - *Can you punish it?* No. Hoarding doses carried reputational cost and nothing
    else.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes — but each government faced an electorate on a
    much shorter clock than the pandemic.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* The US, EU and India each controlled
    enough manufacturing to change global supply, and each acted domestically
    first.
  - *Substitutes?* No. Doses were the binding constraint and there was nothing
    else to give.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Did it ever work?* Session 2, *The games* — a
  textbook n-player prisoner's dilemma with full observability, which is useful
  precisely because it shows observability alone is not sufficient.
  Session 6, *The first enlargement with no outside*.
- **Media:** Our World in Data's vaccination-by-income-group charts are the
  standard visualization and show the gap starkly. Gavi published a preliminary
  impact assessment.
- **Source:** WHO, "COVID-19 vaccinations shift to regular immunization as COVAX
  draws to a close", 19 December 2023. Gavi, "A preliminary assessment of COVAX's
  impact in lower-income countries". *The Conversation*, "Covax misses its 2021
  delivery target", September 2021.

### The WHO Pandemic Agreement

- **What happened:** The World Health Assembly adopted the Pandemic Agreement on
  20 May 2025 by consensus, after a vote the previous day of 124 in favor, none
  against, 11 abstentions. The US did not take part, having begun withdrawal from
  the WHO. The agreement opens for signature only once the Pathogen Access and
  Benefit-Sharing annex is adopted, and enters into force after 60 ratifications.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** An attempt to fix the COVAX failure
  structurally — trading pathogen sample sharing, which developing countries
  supply, against vaccine access, which they need. It is a negotiated exchange
  rather than an appeal to solidarity, which is what makes it interesting.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* 190-odd, minus the largest.
  - *Can you see defection?* Sample sharing is observable. Manufacturing
    allocation commitments, less so.
  - *Can you punish it?* Minimally. Withholding future samples is the main lever,
    and it is the one Indonesia used in 2007 over H5N1.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* The US absence is material: it holds a
    large share of the relevant manufacturing and R&D.
  - *Substitutes?* Not applicable.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Changing the payoffs*, on constructing an exchange
  where an appeal to the common good failed. Session 6, *Why the current stack
  can't hold*.
- **Media:** Primary text at who.int. KFF's explainer is the clearest neutral
  summary of what it does and doesn't do.
- **Source:** PAHO/WHO, "World Health Assembly adopts historic Pandemic
  Agreement", 20 May 2025. KFF, "The 'Pandemic Agreement': What it is, What it
  isn't".

## Fisheries and the ocean

### The Newfoundland cod collapse

- **What happened:** Canada declared a moratorium on the northern cod fishery on
  2 July 1992, after the stock collapsed. More than 30,000 people lost their work
  and the province's population fell by around 10%. Expected to last 2 years, the
  moratorium ran 32. The stock has stabilized at roughly 12% of its 1960s biomass.
  Canada reopened a limited commercial fishery in June 2024 with an 18,000 tonne
  quota, a decision marine scientists contested as premature.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The canonical modern tragedy of the
  commons, with the additional detail that it happened inside a single country's
  200-mile exclusive economic zone, under active government management, with
  scientific advice available throughout. Sovereignty and science were both
  present and it collapsed anyway.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* Thousands of inshore fishers plus a small number of
    offshore trawler fleets — two groups with very different interests, which is
    part of why regulation failed.
  - *Can you see defection?* Poorly, at the time. At-sea catch was
    under-reported and discarding was routine.
  - *Can you punish it?* Yes in principle. In practice quota enforcement was weak
    and the political cost of cutting quotas was borne by the minister who cut
    them.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes — these were multi-generational fishing
    communities, exactly the conditions Ostrom identifies as favorable. They
    weren't enough against a regulator setting quotas above scientific advice.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* Canada was. It had the jurisdiction and
    did not use it in time.
  - *Substitutes?* No. There was no other fishery for those boats, and no other
    employment in those towns. Zero substitutes is why the political economy ran
    one way.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Ostrom and the commons*, as the counter-case to the
  Maine lobster fishery. Session 2, *The games*. The scale-pair with lobster
  grounds named in the arc.
- **Media:** Strong archival footage exists — the July 1992 Radisson Hotel
  announcement in St. John's, where fishers broke down the doors, is on CBC
  Archives. *Hand.Line.Cod* (2016, NFB) is a short documentary on the inshore
  fishery. The DFO stock assessment charts are clean and dramatic.
- **Source:** Fisheries and Oceans Canada, "The Government of Canada announces the
  historic return of the commercial Northern cod fishery", June 2024.
  Newfoundland and Labrador Heritage, "Cod Moratorium". CBC News, "End of cod
  moratorium touted after 32 years", June 2024.

### The IWC commercial whaling moratorium

- **What happened:** The International Whaling Commission voted in 1982 for a
  pause in commercial whaling from the 1985/86 season. Norway lodged a formal
  objection and has never observed it. Japan withdrew its objection in 1985 under
  US trade pressure, stopped commercial whaling in 1988, and then conducted
  large-scale "scientific" whaling under Article VIII for three decades. Iceland
  resumed commercial whaling in 2007. Japan left the IWC entirely in 2019 and
  resumed commercial whaling in its own waters in July 2019. Several great whale
  populations have nonetheless recovered substantially.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A commons with an explicit opt-out clause
  written into the treaty. It shows what happens when defection is legal,
  observable and costs nothing — and also that the outcome can still be good, if
  the number of committed defectors is small enough.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* Around 88 IWC members, three of which actually whale
    commercially.
  - *Can you see defection?* Yes, completely. Whaling fleets are large and catch
    is reported. Sea Shepherd filmed it.
  - *Can you punish it?* Weakly, and only by one route: the US Pelly Amendment
    allows trade sanctions for undermining international fishery conservation.
    The threat moved Japan in 1985 and was not carried through later.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes, annually, which produced three decades of
    stalemate rather than convergence.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* The US was, via market access, and chose
    not to press it.
  - *Substitutes?* Yes, and this is why the case ended better than it looked.
    Whale oil had been replaced by petroleum decades earlier and whale meat demand
    in Japan collapsed on its own. The economics defected from the whalers.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Did it ever work?*, as the ambiguous case between
  Montreal and Kyoto. Session 3, *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem*, on
  punishment that is available but not used.
- **Media:** *The Cove* (2009, Academy Award for Best Documentary) — dolphins in
  Taiji rather than whaling proper, but it's the film people have seen.
  *Whale Wars* (Animal Planet, 2008–2015) documents the Southern Ocean
  confrontations. IWC publishes full catch statistics back to 1985.
- **Source:** IWC, "Commercial Whaling" (iwc.int). *Marine Policy*, "Japan's
  withdrawal from the IWC: An explanation", 2025. Australian Institute of
  International Affairs, "When Shaming Fails: Japanese Withdrawal from the
  International Whaling Commission".

### The WTO Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies

- **What happened:** Adopted at the WTO's 12th Ministerial Conference in June 2022
  after more than 20 years of negotiation, banning subsidies for illegal,
  unreported and unregulated fishing, for fishing overfished stocks, and for
  fishing unregulated high seas. It entered into force on 15 September 2025 once
  two-thirds of members had deposited acceptances. A sunset clause terminates the
  agreement if comprehensive additional disciplines are not adopted within 4 years
  of entry into force — so by September 2029.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A race to the bottom rather than a
  commons problem directly. Each state subsidizes its fleet to keep its share of a
  shrinking catch, and the subsidies collectively fund the overfishing. The first
  WTO agreement with an environmental objective at its core, and the first
  multilateral agreement concluded at the WTO since 2013.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* 160-odd WTO members; subsidies are heavily concentrated in
    about ten.
  - *Can you see defection?* Improving. Subsidy notification is required, and
    satellite vessel-tracking (Global Fishing Watch) has made fleet behavior
    observable in a way it was not ten years ago. This is a genuine technological
    change in question 2.
  - *Can you punish it?* In principle yes — WTO dispute settlement. In practice
    the Appellate Body has been non-functional since 2019, which weakens it
    considerably.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes, and the sunset clause makes the next round
    compulsory.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* No.
  - *Substitutes?* Partly — aquaculture has grown enormously and now supplies
    more than half of fish for human consumption, which reduces the pressure
    behind the subsidies.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Did it ever work?* — a recent and genuine
  multilateral success, useful against the assumption that nothing gets agreed
  any more. Session 4, *Changing the payoffs*.
- **Media:** Global Fishing Watch's public map is the best live data visualization
  available for any case in this list. WTO holds the text and the acceptance
  register.
- **Source:** WTO, "WTO Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies enters into force",
  15 September 2025. Pew Charitable Trusts, "Fisheries Subsidies Agreement: Why
  It Matters", October 2025.

### The High Seas Treaty (BBNJ)

- **What happened:** Adopted 19 June 2023 after nearly two decades of negotiation,
  covering conservation and sustainable use of marine biodiversity beyond national
  jurisdiction — roughly two-thirds of the ocean, previously without a mechanism
  for creating protected areas. The 60th ratification was deposited on
  19 September 2025 and the agreement enters into force on 17 January 2026.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The largest unowned commons on the
  planet, and the first instrument that lets anyone enclose parts of it. Too new
  to judge, which is worth saying rather than treating its adoption as the
  outcome.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* All states, with a small number of distant-water fishing
    nations holding most of the activity.
  - *Can you see defection?* Better than before, through satellite AIS tracking —
    though vessels can and do go dark.
  - *Can you punish it?* Flag-state enforcement only, which is the structural
    weakness: flags of convenience exist precisely to sell the absence of
    enforcement.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes — a Conference of the Parties is established.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* No.
  - *Substitutes?* Not applicable.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Ostrom and the commons*, on enclosure as one of the
  available answers. Session 6, *Why the current stack can't hold*, since
  flag-state enforcement is exactly the sovereignty-shaped hole.
- **Media:** High Seas Alliance ratification tracker is live and well designed.
  No documentary known.
- **Source:** High Seas Alliance, "60th ratification triggers entry into force of
  High Seas Treaty", 19 September 2025. IISD SDG Knowledge Hub, "With 60
  Ratifications, BBNJ Agreement to Enter into Force in January 2026".

## Information commons and technical coordination

### Wikipedia

- **What happened:** Founded January 2001. English Wikipedia now holds over 7
  million articles and received 62 million edits in 2025 (172 million across all
  languages). Active editors peaked above 51,000 in 2007 and have declined to
  around 39,000 as of December 2024, while article count and length keep growing.
  It is funded by donation and has no paid editorial staff.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A positive commons — a public good that
  should be massively undersupplied by Olson's logic and is not. Understanding why
  it works matters more for this course than any individual failure case.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* Millions of potential contributors; a few tens of
    thousands doing most of the work. The core group is small, which is the
    Olsonian answer.
  - *Can you see defection?* Yes, completely and immediately. Every edit is
    attributed, timestamped and diffable, and the revision history is permanent.
    This is the most observable system in this list.
  - *Can you punish it?* Yes — reversion, page protection, blocks, bans, and an
    arbitration committee. A full governance stack grew organically.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes, and reputation is persistent and portable within
    the project.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* No, and the project's design assumes it.
  - *Substitutes?* Nupedia and Citizendium existed and failed; Wikipedia's
    advantage was the absence of a credible substitute once network effects took
    hold. The live question now is whether LLMs trained on it substitute for it,
    and what happens to contribution when the readership goes.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Ostrom and the commons* — Wikipedia satisfies most
  of the design principles and is the best modern instance of them at scale.
  Session 1, *Introduction to collective action*, as an opening success.
  Session 4, *Polycentricity*, on the language-community federation.
- **Media:** Wikimedia Statistics (stats.wikimedia.org) is a public, live,
  well-built dashboard — genuinely good for live demonstration. *Truth in Numbers?*
  (2010) is a documentary about the project. Every article's history page is a
  primary source you can open in front of an audience.
- **Source:** Wikimedia Foundation statistics, stats.wikimedia.org. Aaron
  Halfaker et al., "The Rise and Decline of an Open Collaboration System",
  *American Behavioral Scientist* 57, 2013.

### Open source infrastructure and the maintainer problem

- **What happened:** Heartbleed was disclosed in April 2014, a flaw in OpenSSL,
  which secured roughly two-thirds of web servers and was maintained by a handful
  of volunteers with one person full-time and an annual budget of around $2,000.
  Industry responded with the Core Infrastructure Initiative, funding two full-time
  developers. Log4Shell followed in December 2021, in a logging library maintained
  by a small volunteer team. In March 2024 Andres Freund found a backdoor in xz
  utils (CVE-2024-3094, CVSS 10) planted by a contributor who had spent roughly two
  years building trust with an overloaded maintainer before being given commit
  rights.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A positive commons underprovided in the
  textbook pattern — every firm depends on it, none pays, and the free-riding is
  near-total. The xz case adds a second structure on top: the maintainer burnout
  produced by the underfunding is itself the attack surface.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* Every software-using organization on earth. Vast N, and
    the good is genuinely non-excludable.
  - *Can you see defection?* Not meaningfully. There is no register of who depends
    on what, and not funding a project is invisible.
  - *Can you punish it?* No. Licenses explicitly permit use without contribution.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes, but anonymously.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* Yes, and this is how it partly gets
    solved. Google, Microsoft and others fund critical projects because they
    individually benefit enough — Olson's privileged group, doing exactly what the
    theory says.
  - *Substitutes?* No. The dependency is the point.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Olson* — the privileged group and selective
  incentives, in a case the audience uses every day. Session 3, *Ostrom and the
  commons*, on the positive/subtractable distinction. Session 1,
  *Introduction to collective action*.
- **Media:** The XKCD 2347 "Dependency" cartoon is the single most effective
  visual for this in existence and needs no explanation. Freund's disclosure email
  to oss-security (29 March 2024) is a readable primary source. Nadia Eghbal's
  *Roads and Bridges* (Ford Foundation, 2016) is the standard report.
- **Source:** OpenSSF, "xz Backdoor CVE-2024-3094", 30 March 2024. Nadia Eghbal,
  *Roads and Bridges: The Unseen Labor Behind Our Digital Infrastructure*, Ford
  Foundation, 2016. *Washington Post*, "In wake of Heartbleed, tech companies
  commit millions to critical open-source software", April 2014.

### Y2K

- **What happened:** Two-digit year fields in legacy systems threatened
  date-handling failures at the century rollover. The US Department of Commerce
  put total US remediation cost at $100 billion over 1995–2001. Coordination ran
  through national councils, an International Y2K Cooperation Center, and
  sector-by-sector information sharing including between direct competitors.
  Essentially nothing failed.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Interdependent remediation — your systems
  are only safe if your suppliers' and your counterparties' systems are too, so
  the incentive to disclose your own vulnerability status was low and disclosure
  happened anyway. And it is the cleanest available example of the problem that
  successful prevention is indistinguishable from there having been no problem,
  which has made Y2K a byword for hysteria.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* Every organization with a computer, but the binding
    dependencies were in a few sectors — banking, power, telecoms, aviation —
    each with a small number of large players and an existing regulator.
  - *Can you see defection?* Yes, through mandated disclosure: US public companies
    were required to report Y2K readiness to the SEC, and regulators audited banks
    and utilities directly.
  - *Can you punish it?* Yes — regulatory action, and liability. The 1998 Year
    2000 Information and Readiness Disclosure Act went the other way, granting
    limited immunity to encourage firms to share what they knew.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes, and the deadline was fixed, public and known years
    in advance — an unusually favorable feature.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* No, but sector regulators were, and used
    it.
  - *Substitutes?* Not applicable. There was one fix and it had to be done.
- **Could serve:** Session 1, *Introduction to collective action*, as a success
  where the success is invisible — which also sets up why the record of
  cooperation looks worse than it is. Session 4, *Did it ever work?*
- **Media:** Extensive news archive footage of the rollover night. The Commerce
  Department report "The Economics of Y2K and the Impact on the United States" is
  online. No good documentary known.
- **Source:** US Department of Commerce, *The Economics of Y2K and the Impact on
  the United States*, 1999. Government Executive, "Y2K costs government,
  businesses $100 billion", November 1999.

### Internet standards: IPv6, DNSSEC, RPKI

- **What happened:** Three coordination problems in the same system, with three
  different outcomes. IPv6 was specified in 1998; Google measured native IPv6
  access crossing 50% of its users for the first time in March 2026, 18 years
  after it began tracking. DNSSEC, specified in the 1990s, remains at roughly 4%
  of domains signed and 13% resolver validation as of early 2026. RPKI route
  origin validation, a much later effort, reached 67% of announced prefixes by
  June 2026, although only 12% of autonomous systems fully validate.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Pure coordination problems with network
  externalities — deploying early costs you and benefits everyone else, and the
  benefit to you arrives only when others deploy. Three variants of the same
  structure with sharply different adoption curves, which is what makes this
  worth a slide rather than a sentence.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* Tens of thousands of network operators, no central
    authority, standards set by an open body (IETF) with no enforcement power at
    all.
  - *Can you see defection?* Completely. Deployment is publicly measurable and
    several organizations publish live per-network scoreboards.
  - *Can you punish it?* No, and yet adoption happens. Naming and ranking is the
    only lever, plus procurement requirements from large buyers.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes, permanently and visibly — operator communities
    (NANOG, RIPE) are small, repeated and reputationally dense.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* Partly. Cloudflare, Google and the large
    clouds can shift a measured percentage by themselves, and did so for RPKI.
  - *Substitutes?* Yes, and this explains the difference between the three. NAT
    was a good enough substitute for IPv6 addresses, which is why IPv6 took
    eighteen years. There was no comparable substitute for route-origin
    validation once BGP hijacks became routine, and RPKI moved fast.
- **Could serve:** Session 2, *The games*, as a coordination problem rather than a
  dilemma — the distinction the module is built on, with real data attached.
  Session 4, *Polycentricity*, since internet governance is the working example of
  stacked authority with no sovereign.
- **Media:** Live dashboards: Google IPv6 statistics, ISOC Pulse, NIST RPKI
  Monitor, APNIC DNSSEC world map. All public, all updating, all good for
  demonstrating in a session.
- **Source:** Google IPv6 statistics (google.com/intl/en/ipv6/statistics.html).
  Internet Society Pulse, "18 Years Later, IPv6 Reaches Majority", April 2026.
  Kentik, "Exploring the Latest RPKI ROV Adoption Numbers".

### Radio spectrum and the ITU

- **What happened:** Spectrum is allocated internationally through World
  Radiocommunication Conferences, held roughly every 4 years, which revise the ITU
  Radio Regulations — a binding international treaty. WRC-23 met in Dubai in
  November–December 2023; WRC-27 is next. The system has run continuously since
  the 1906 Berlin conference and has coordinated a genuinely rival resource across
  every state on earth for over a century, largely without incident.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A subtractable commons where physics
  enforces rivalry — two transmitters on the same frequency in the same place
  destroy each other's signal. Arguably the oldest continuously functioning
  global commons regime, and nearly invisible because it works.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* 190-odd administrations, plus satellite operators.
  - *Can you see defection?* Yes, immediately and unambiguously. Interference is
    self-announcing and can be geolocated. This is the strongest yes on question 2
    in the entire list.
  - *Can you punish it?* Formally weak — the ITU has no sanction. In practice,
    interference is reciprocal, so defection punishes itself automatically.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes, on a fixed multi-year cycle with continuous
    technical committee work between.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* No, and it doesn't matter, because
    nobody benefits from acting alone.
  - *Substitutes?* Partly. Technical change — spread spectrum, frequency reuse,
    higher bands, fiber — keeps expanding the effective resource, which
    repeatedly defuses allocation fights before they harden.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Did it ever work?*, as the boring successful case
  that nobody cites. Session 3, *Ostrom and the commons*. Session 2, *The games*,
  on coordination versus dilemma — spectrum is mostly coordination, which is why
  it's tractable.
- **Media:** ITU publishes WRC documentation and frequency allocation charts; the
  US NTIA spectrum allocation chart is a famous and genuinely striking poster.
  No documentary known.
- **Source:** ITU Radio Regulations and WRC-23 Final Acts (itu.int). Hazlett,
  *The Political Spectrum*, Yale, 2017, for the critical account.

## Economy and attention

### Tax havens and the global minimum tax

- **What happened:** Decades of tax competition drove statutory corporate rates
  down globally and moved reported profit to low-tax jurisdictions. The OECD/G20
  Inclusive Framework agreed Pillar Two in 2021, a 15% global minimum effective
  tax on multinationals with turnover above €750m, implemented through top-up
  taxes that a home or intermediate jurisdiction collects if the source
  jurisdiction does not. Rules began applying in 2024 across the EU, UK, Japan,
  South Korea and others. In January 2026 the OECD released a "side-by-side"
  package allowing US-parented groups to elect out of the main charging rules from
  fiscal years beginning on or after 1 January 2026, following G7 agreement in
  2025.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A textbook race to the bottom, with a
  clever structural fix — the top-up tax makes defection unprofitable for the
  haven, because the revenue is collected elsewhere if the haven doesn't collect
  it. Then the largest player negotiated an exemption, which is the part worth
  teaching.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* 140-odd jurisdictions in the Inclusive Framework; a small
    number of havens hold most of the shifted profit.
  - *Can you see defection?* Much better than before. Country-by-country reporting
    since 2016 made profit shifting visible for the first time.
  - *Can you punish it?* Yes, and this is the innovation. The under-taxed profits
    rule means non-participation doesn't protect the haven's revenue base — a
    design that makes the agreement self-enforcing rather than sanction-dependent.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* The US is, and demonstrated it in 2025–26
    by obtaining a carve-out rather than complying.
  - *Substitutes?* Not applicable directly; the relevant question is whether
    havens have another revenue model, and mostly they don't, which is why they
    resisted.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Changing the payoffs* — the best modern example of
  redesigning a game so that defection stops paying. Session 2, *The many names*
  (race to the bottom). Session 4, *Did it ever work?* for the qualification.
- **Media:** The EU Tax Observatory's *Global Tax Evasion Report* has good
  profit-shifting visualizations. Zucman's *The Hidden Wealth of Nations* (2015)
  is the accessible book.
- **Source:** OECD, *Global Anti-Base Erosion Model Rules (Pillar Two)*, 2021.
  OECD, Minimum Tax Implementation Handbook. Gabriel Zucman, *The Hidden Wealth
  of Nations*, Chicago, 2015.

### Attention, social media and the under-16 age limit

- **What happened:** Australia's Online Safety Amendment (Social Media Minimum
  Age) took effect on 10 December 2025, requiring platforms including YouTube, X,
  Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, Snapchat, Reddit, Twitch, Threads and Kick to take
  reasonable steps to prevent under-16s holding accounts, with penalties up to
  A$49.5m. Denmark, Malaysia, New Zealand and the European Parliament have moved
  in the same direction since; the European Parliament passed a non-binding
  resolution favoring a minimum age of 16 in November 2025.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Adolescent social media use is a
  collective action trap at the level of individuals — no teenager gains by leaving
  a platform their friends are on, and no parent gains by unilaterally banning it.
  This is the structure Haidt calls a collective action problem explicitly, and
  the legislation is an attempt to solve it by coercion rather than persuasion,
  which is a Hobbes/Hardin move applied to a social norm.
- **The diagnostic:**
  - *How many players?* Millions of families, which is why the norm approach
    fails; a dozen platforms, which is why the regulatory approach might not.
  - *Can you see defection?* Between families, yes and visibly, which is exactly
    the pressure that sustains the trap. Platform compliance with age assurance is
    much harder to verify.
  - *Can you punish it?* Now yes, for platforms. Not for individuals, nor should
    it be.
  - *Will you meet again?* Yes, continuously — school communities are dense
    repeated-interaction environments, and coordinated phone-free school policies
    are the local version of the same fix.
  - *Is anyone big enough to act alone?* No individual family. One national
    government can act, and Australia is testing whether one is enough to start a
    cascade.
  - *Substitutes?* Partly — the question is whether there's an alternative social
    infrastructure for teenagers, and mostly there isn't, which is what makes
    unilateral exit so costly.
- **Could serve:** Session 1, *The pattern beneath the metacrisis* — this is the
  case that connects the structure to the audience's own life, and the arc names
  exactly this ("it makes sense to me to sit in my room and watch Netflix").
  Session 4, *Coercion*. Session 2, *The games*.
- **Contested:** The empirical claim that social media causes the adolescent
  mental health decline is disputed — Haidt argues it, Odgers and Przybylski
  dispute the strength of the evidence. The collective action structure holds
  independently of who wins that argument, and it's worth separating the two in
  front of an audience that will have opinions.
- **Media:** Australian government fact sheets and eSafety Commissioner guidance
  are primary. Haidt's *The Anxious Generation* (2024) is the popular text and
  makes the collective action argument explicitly. Good coverage in CSMonitor and
  CNBC, December 2025.
- **Source:** Australian Department of Infrastructure, "Social media minimum age".
  Jonathan Haidt, *The Anxious Generation*, Penguin, 2024. Candice Odgers, "The
  great rewiring: is social media really behind an epidemic of teenage mental
  illness?", *Nature* 628, 2024.

## Notes on coverage

Two things the list is short on, deliberately flagged rather than padded:

- **Non-Western cases.** Nearly everything here is a treaty negotiated in or an
  industry based in North America, Europe or East Asia. The ASEAN transboundary
  haze agreement and the Nile Basin Initiative would both fit the diagnostic and
  neither has been researched here.
- **Successes at intermediate scale.** The list jumps from Wikipedia to global
  treaties. City- and region-level cases — congestion charging in London,
  Stockholm and Singapore, where a coordination problem was solved by a single
  authority against initial public opposition and then became popular — would fill
  the gap between Ostrom's villages and the planet.
