# Warfare and what makes groups hold together — candidate examples

Candidates only. Nothing here is written up for teaching yet.

Scope: this list is about cohesion *inside* a fighting group — what makes men stand
in a line, and what happens when they stop. Cooperation *across* the lines — trench
truces, the Christmas truce, rescue under occupation — is in the "Cooperation under
war" section of [examples-historical-successes.md](examples-historical-successes.md)
and is not repeated here.

Two warnings that apply throughout.

Ancient warfare has unusually unreliable popular accounts and unusually contested
scholarship. The mechanics of the hoplite push are a live academic fight, and the
numbers at Thermopylae vary by more than an order of magnitude between ancient and
modern sources. Disputes are flagged in the entry.

The modern combat-motivation literature has a contamination problem. S.L.A.
Marshall's fire-ratio finding, which is the single most repeated claim in the
field, was not researched the way he said it was. It is flagged below and should
not be used.

## The phalanx: the structure of a line that holds

### The hoplite shield wall and the rightward drift

- **What happened:** Greek heavy infantry from roughly the 7th to the 4th century
  BC fought shoulder to shoulder in a formation several ranks deep, each man
  carrying a round shield (*aspis*) about 90cm across on his left arm. Thucydides,
  describing the run-up to the battle of Mantinea in 418 BC, records that armies
  always drift rightward as they advance, "because fear makes each man do his best
  to shelter his unarmed side with the shield of the man next to him on the
  right".
- **Why it's a collective action case:** This is the structural heart of the
  section, and Thucydides supplies the mechanism himself. The shield covers its
  bearer's left side and overlaps the man to his left, so protection is produced
  *by* the formation and consumed by your neighbor. Nobody can protect himself; the
  line protects you or nothing does. That makes cohesion a public good with a
  particularly brutal payoff structure — one man running does not reduce the good
  marginally, it opens a hole and can collapse the whole thing. The mechanism
  holding it together is **mutual hostage-taking plus total observability**: your
  own survival is in the hands of the two men either side of you, and they can see
  exactly what you are doing, because they are touching you. The rightward drift is
  the tell that individual incentives are pulling against the formation even while
  it holds.
- **Could serve:** *Introduction to collective action* (S1), as the cleanest
  physical image of a public good in the course; *War* (S1); *Olson* (S3), on why
  small groups work.
- **Media:** Extensive vase painting, particularly the Chigi Vase (c. 640 BC) which
  shows ranked hoplites. Reenactment groups (Hoplologia, the Hoplite Association)
  have produced experimental footage. The Bardo and Athens museums hold surviving
  shield facings.
- **Source:** Thucydides, *History of the Peloponnesian War* 5.71; Victor Davis
  Hanson, *The Western Way of War*, 2nd ed. (University of California Press, 2009);
  Hans van Wees, *Greek Warfare: Myths and Realities* (Duckworth, 2004).

### The othismos dispute

- **What happened:** Greek sources use the word *othismos* — "pushing" — for a phase
  of hoplite battle. What physically happened is disputed and has been for about a
  century.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** It matters for teaching because the two
  readings imply different mechanisms. On the orthodox reading, associated with
  Victor Davis Hanson, the rear ranks physically shoved the men in front, like a
  rugby scrum, so a man in the front rank literally could not run — cohesion
  enforced by the mass behind him. On the alternative reading, argued by Adrian
  Goldsworthy and George Cawkwell, *othismos* is metaphorical or a late-stage surge
  after a period of individual fighting, which makes staying in line a choice
  rather than a physical inevitability. Cawkwell called the literal scrum reading
  "wildest folly", on the grounds that the front ranks would have been unable to use
  their weapons at all.
- **Contested:** This is the dispute, not a case with a dispute attached. No ancient
  source explicitly describes all ranks packing together and driving forward, so
  both readings are inferences. Use it as an illustration of how thin the evidence
  is for mechanisms that everyone thinks they know.
- **Could serve:** *War* (S1), if the course wants to show that the mechanism is the
  contested part; *Introduction to collective action* (S1).
- **Media:** Experimental archaeology footage exists from several reenactment
  groups; treat it as illustration, not evidence.
- **Source:** A. K. Goldsworthy, "The *Othismos*, Myths and Heresies: The Nature of
  Hoplite Battle", *War in History* 4 (1997); Hanson, *The Western Way of War*;
  Peter Krentz, "The Nature of Hoplite Battle", *Classical Antiquity* 4 (1985).

### Marathon and Plataea

- **What happened:** At Marathon in 490 BC an Athenian and Plataean citizen army
  defeated a Persian invasion force on the coast of Attica. At Plataea in 479 BC a
  combined Greek army under Spartan command defeated the Persian field army in
  Boeotia, ending the invasion. Both are hoplite battles fought by men who were
  farmers and craftsmen the month before and after.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The mechanism worth naming is **conscription
  from a face-to-face community into the unit that fights**. Athenian hoplites
  served by tribe (*phylē*), so the man beside you in the line was a neighbor you
  would see in the assembly and the marketplace for the rest of your life, and your
  conduct would be reported there. That converts a one-shot terror decision into a
  repeated game with an audience — questions 2 and 4 of the diagnostic, supplied by
  the recruitment system rather than by anything done on the day.
- **Contested:** Numbers for both battles, and particularly Persian numbers, are
  unreliable — see the Thermopylae entry.
- **Could serve:** *War* (S1); *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem* (S3);
  *The cooperative ape* (S3).
- **Media:** The Marathon tumulus (Soros) still stands and is visitable. The
  Serpent Column from Delphi, listing the Greek states that fought at Plataea, is in
  the Hippodrome in Istanbul — a good primary object.
- **Source:** Herodotus, *Histories* 6.102–117 (Marathon) and 9.19–70 (Plataea);
  Peter Krentz, *The Battle of Marathon* (Yale UP, 2010).

### Thermopylae, 480 BC

- **What happened:** A Greek force held the pass at Thermopylae against the Persian
  army for some days before being outflanked by a mountain path. Leonidas dismissed
  most of the allied contingents and remained with the Spartans, the Thespians and
  the Thebans, and was killed.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The interesting part is the dismissal, not
  the last stand. A rearguard has an obvious free-rider structure — everyone is
  better off if someone stays and better off still if it isn't them — and the
  historical answer was not persuasion but selection in advance. Herodotus says
  Leonidas' 300 were chosen from men who had living sons, which removes the
  strongest private reason to survive and is as close to a designed selective
  incentive as the ancient record gets. The Spartan case also runs on the reverse
  side: see *tresantes* below, where the penalty for surviving a defeat was social
  death.
- **Contested:** Heavily, and in the direction the popular account runs. The force
  was not 300 — modern reconstructions of the Greek total run to around 7,000, with
  Thespians and Thebans dying alongside the Spartiates, plus perioikoi and helots
  usually left out of the retelling; Diodorus gives 1,000 Lacedaemonians rather than
  300. Herodotus' Persian figures (over 2 million) are not credible; modern
  estimates range from roughly 100,000 to 300,000 and are themselves contested. Say
  this plainly if the case is used, because the audience will arrive with the film
  version.
- **Could serve:** *War* (S1); *The bill* (S5), on what cohesion is manufactured for.
- **Media:** *300* (Zack Snyder, 2006) is the version everyone knows and is a comic
  adaptation, not a historical one; use only as the thing being corrected. The 1962
  *The 300 Spartans* is closer to Herodotus. The Kolonos hill and the Simonides
  epigram are on site.
- **Source:** Herodotus, *Histories* 7.201–233; Diodorus Siculus 11.4; Paul
  Cartledge, *Thermopylae: The Battle That Changed the World* (Macmillan, 2006).

### Socrates as a hoplite

- **What happened:** Socrates served in the Athenian heavy infantry at the siege of
  Potidaea (432 BC), at Delium (424 BC) and reportedly at Amphipolis (422 BC). In
  Plato's *Symposium*, Alcibiades says Socrates saved his life and his arms at
  Potidaea and then let the prize for valor be awarded to Alcibiades instead. Of
  Delium, Alcibiades says he watched the retreat from horseback while Socrates was
  on foot, and that Socrates withdrew so composedly — "looking about at friends and
  enemies", in the usual rendering — that pursuers went after the men who were
  running instead. In the *Laches*, the general Laches gives a corroborating
  eyewitness account.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The mechanism is **orderly withdrawal as a
  coordination problem**, and it is the exact inverse of the rout entries below.
  Men who retreat in formation are hard to kill; men who run are easy. Each
  individual's dominant move is to run, and the result of everyone taking it is that
  most of them die. Socrates' reported conduct is interesting precisely because
  Alcibiades reports that it *worked* — composure was legible to the enemy as
  continued cohesion, and pursuit went elsewhere.
- **Contested:** These are literary portraits in philosophical dialogues written by
  a partisan, not military dispatches, and Alcibiades' speech in the *Symposium* is
  a set-piece encomium. Delium was an Athenian defeat and a genuine rout; that much
  is independently in Thucydides. Treat the detail of Socrates' bearing as Plato's
  characterization.
- **Could serve:** *War* (S1); *Introduction to collective action* (S1) — a small,
  human-scale entry point to the rout problem.
- **Media:** Plato's texts are short, public domain and readable aloud — *Symposium*
  219e–221c is the passage. S. Sara Monoson's chapter is the scholarly treatment.
- **Source:** Plato, *Symposium* 219e–221c and *Laches* 181a–b; Thucydides 4.89–101
  on Delium; S. Sara Monoson, "Socrates' Military Service", in *Socrates and
  Dionysus* (2019).

### The Sacred Band of Thebes

- **What happened:** An elite Theban unit of 300, formed in the 370s BC and, on
  Plutarch's account, made up of 150 pairs of male lovers. It was central to the
  Theban defeat of Sparta at Leuctra in 371 BC and was destroyed at Chaeronea in
  338 BC by Philip II of Macedon. Nineteenth-century excavation under the Lion of
  Chaeronea found 254 skeletons laid out in rows, generally identified as the Band.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** An explicit, deliberate attempt to engineer
  cohesion by **making the unit out of pre-existing bonds rather than hoping bonds
  form inside it**. Plutarch reports the reasoning directly: a man will abandon a
  stranger and will not abandon a lover, because shame in front of that specific
  person is unbearable. This is the ancestor of the whole primary-group literature,
  stated as design intent 2,300 years earlier, and it is worth putting next to Shils
  and Janowitz for exactly that reason.
- **Contested:** The Band is not mentioned in any source before Dinarchus in the 320s
  BC, and Plutarch writes some four centuries after Chaeronea, so the pairing detail
  rests on late testimony. The identification of the Chaeronea skeletons is
  generally accepted but is an inference from location.
- **Could serve:** *War* (S1); *Ritual and the manufactured tribe* (S5), as a
  secular version of the same move; *Olson* (S3).
- **Media:** The Lion of Chaeronea stands on the site and is photographed
  extensively. James Romm, *The Sacred Band* (Scribner, 2021) is the accessible
  recent account.
- **Source:** Plutarch, *Life of Pelopidas* 18–19; Plato, *Symposium* 178e–179a, for
  the "army of lovers" idea Plutarch is drawing on; Romm, *The Sacred Band* (2021).

### The Plataean breakout, winter 428/427 BC

- **What happened:** Besieged Plataeans, walled in by a Peloponnesian
  circumvallation, planned an escape over the wall. They built ladders to match the
  wall's height by counting its courses of brick from a distance — many men counting
  at once, on the reasoning that although some would miscount, the most common
  answer would be right. Around 220 of roughly 480 men who had originally agreed went
  over on a stormy night; 212 reached Athens.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Two mechanisms in one short episode, and it
  is the best siege-and-sortie candidate on the list. First, **aggregation as a
  substitute for measurement** — an early and explicit use of independent errors
  cancelling, which is a coordination solution rather than a dilemma solution.
  Second, the participation decision: roughly half the men who had agreed to go
  lost their nerve at the last moment and stayed. The escape got *safer* the fewer
  went, since a smaller party is quieter, which makes this a rare military case where
  free riding does not degrade the good — and the split still fell near half.
- **Could serve:** *Introduction to collective action* (S1); *The games* (S2), as a
  coordination problem; *Olson* (S3).
- **Media:** None known in film. Thucydides 3.20–24 is short and vivid enough to read
  aloud.
- **Source:** Thucydides, *History of the Peloponnesian War* 3.20–24.

## Manufacturing cohesion: Sparta and Rome

### The Spartan agoge

- **What happened:** Spartan boys were removed from their households at around age
  seven into a state-run upbringing organized in age classes, with communal living,
  deliberate deprivation, competition, ritual theft and public beatings, continuing
  into adulthood. This is the description given by Xenophon and, much later and much
  more fully, by Plutarch.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The most thoroughgoing historical attempt to
  **manufacture a primary group by destroying the competing one**. Kin loyalty is
  the main rival to unit loyalty, so the system removed the child from the family and
  substituted an age cohort he would later fight beside. That is the same structural
  move Henrich attributes to the medieval Church's marriage rules — dissolve kin
  networks to make a larger unit possible — run by a state for military purposes.
- **Contested:** Seriously. Nigel Kennell argued that the *agoge* as Plutarch
  describes it is largely a Hellenistic and Roman-era reinvention, archaizing
  features included, produced to support Sparta's claim to be a unique society. The
  word *agoge* itself is not securely attested for the classical system. The
  classical-period evidence is much thinner than the popular picture, and the
  popular picture is downstream of a source writing 500 years later. Stephen
  Hodkinson's work on the wider "Spartan mirage" applies.
- **Could serve:** *Ritual and the manufactured tribe* (S5); *Henrich and the trust
  of strangers* (S5), as the comparison case; *The bill* (S5).
- **Media:** Little reliable visual material; Sparta left almost no monumental
  remains, which is itself a teachable fact. Avoid *300* here.
- **Source:** Xenophon, *Constitution of the Lacedaemonians*; Plutarch, *Life of
  Lycurgus*; Nigel M. Kennell, *The Gymnasium of Virtue: Education and Culture in
  Ancient Sparta* (University of North Carolina Press, 1995); Stephen Hodkinson,
  *Property and Wealth in Classical Sparta* (2000).

### The syssitia

- **What happened:** Spartan citizens ate their evening meal in small common messes
  (*syssitia*, also *phiditia*) rather than at home, for life. Each member had to
  contribute a fixed quota of barley, wine, cheese and figs from his own land;
  failure to contribute cost citizenship. Admission to a mess was by unanimous vote
  of the existing members, on Plutarch's account using a ball dropped into a
  vessel — one dissenter excluded the candidate.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The best single Spartan entry, because the
  mechanism is unusually legible and does several jobs at once. **A daily
  attendance requirement is a cheap, continuous observability device**: absence is
  immediately visible to the fifteen men who will stand beside you in the line. The
  contribution quota is a recurring costly signal with a hard penalty. And the
  unanimity rule on entry means every member has already been individually vouched
  for by every other member, which is a selection mechanism rather than a
  socialization one. Note the ugly corollary — the quota required land, and land
  required helots, so the mess system is directly financed by the subject
  population.
- **Contested:** The blackball detail comes from Plutarch and is late. The wider
  economic argument — that syssitia contributions tied citizenship to landholding
  and so drove the long decline in Spartiate numbers — is Hodkinson's and is
  debated.
- **Could serve:** *Costly signals and Big Gods* (S5); *Ritual and the manufactured
  tribe* (S5); *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem* (S3); *The bill* (S5).
- **Media:** None known.
- **Source:** Plutarch, *Life of Lycurgus* 10–12; Aristotle, *Politics* 1271a; Stephen
  Hodkinson, *Property and Wealth in Classical Sparta* (Classical Press of Wales,
  2000).

### The tresantes — Spartan "tremblers"

- **What happened:** A Spartan who fled in battle, or survived a defeat his comrades
  did not, could be designated a *tresas*, a trembler. The reported consequences
  were civil and social rather than capital: exclusion from office and from the
  messes, ineligibility for marriage, an obligation to give way in the street, a
  duty to wear a patched cloak and shave half the beard, and no legal recourse
  against assault. Herodotus records Aristodemus, who left Thermopylae alive, being
  shunned on return and seeking death at Plataea the following year.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** **Punishment devolved to the whole
  population and made continuous.** A death penalty for cowardice has to be
  administered by someone, costs the state a soldier, and is over quickly. Social
  death is administered by everyone at no cost to anyone, is visible daily, is
  imposed by exactly the people whose regard the sanction operates on, and does not
  end. It is the sharpest historical example of a second-order problem being solved
  by making punishment free.
- **Contested:** Whether the status was permanent, temporary or heritable is not
  established. After Leuctra in 371 BC Plutarch reports Agesilaus being asked what to
  do with the many survivors and answering that the laws should be allowed to sleep
  for a day — which suggests the penalty was unenforceable at scale, and that the
  mechanism only worked on small numbers.
- **Could serve:** *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem* (S3) — punishment;
  *Coercion* (S4); *Ritual and the manufactured tribe* (S5).
- **Media:** None known.
- **Source:** Herodotus, *Histories* 7.229–231 and 9.71; Xenophon, *Constitution of
  the Lacedaemonians* 9; Plutarch, *Life of Agesilaus* 30; Marcello Lupi, "Tremblers
  (*tresantes*) at Sparta", in *The Encyclopedia of Ancient History* (Wiley, 2013).

### The Roman manipular legion

- **What happened:** From roughly the 4th to the 2nd century BC the Roman legion
  fought in maniples — blocks of around 120 men arranged in three lines with gaps,
  *hastati* in front, *principes* behind, *triarii* in reserve — rather than as one
  continuous mass. Polybius, writing in the 2nd century BC, is the main source. The
  arrangement allowed a tired or pressed front line to fall back through the gaps
  and be relieved.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A structural answer to the problem the
  phalanx has no answer to. In an unbroken line, the only exit is to run, and one
  man running can start a rout. The maniple system supplies **a legitimate,
  ordered exit**, so exhaustion and fear do not have to express themselves as
  collapse. It converts an all-or-nothing cohesion problem into a rotation, which is
  the same move as shift work or crop rotation. If the course wants one institutional
  design lesson from ancient warfare, it is this: give people a permitted way to
  stop, or they will find an unpermitted one.
- **Contested:** Exactly how line relief worked in practice — whether the maniples
  physically interchanged, and how the gaps were covered — is debated and not settled
  by Polybius' text.
- **Could serve:** *War* (S1); *Changing the payoffs* (S4), read as institutional
  design; *Introduction to collective action* (S1).
- **Media:** Trajan's Column (AD 113) is later and imperial but is the standard
  visual source for Roman infantry; the Ahenobarbus relief is closer in date.
  Reenactment groups (Ermine Street Guard) produce drill footage.
- **Source:** Polybius, *Histories* 6.19–42; Adrian Goldsworthy, *The Roman Army at
  War, 100 BC–AD 200* (Oxford UP, 1996).

### Decimation and the fustuarium

- **What happened:** Polybius describes the *fustuarium*: a soldier condemned for
  desertion, theft from the camp, false witness or repeated offenses was clubbed or
  stoned to death by his own comrades in front of the assembled army. For collective
  offenses — a unit abandoning its position — Rome used decimation, in which one man
  in ten was selected by lot and killed by the other nine. Crassus is reported by
  Appian and Plutarch to have revived it against troops who fled during the war with
  Spartacus, around 71 BC.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Two distinct mechanisms, and separating them
  is the value of the entry. The *fustuarium* solves the **second-order free rider
  problem by conscripting the punishers**: the men who would otherwise have to be
  paid or coerced into enforcing the norm are made to do the killing themselves, at
  no cost to the state, and are thereby implicated. Decimation is stranger and worth
  dwelling on — it punishes by lot, so it deliberately severs the link between
  individual guilt and individual punishment. What that buys is **collective
  liability**: since you may die for your neighbor's flight, you now have a reason to
  stop him running, which is precisely the incentive a line needs.
- **Contested:** Decimation was rare and treated as archaic even by Romans; the
  reported instances are few and mostly attached to famous names. Do not present it
  as routine practice. The details of Crassus' decimation differ between Appian and
  Plutarch.
- **Could serve:** *Coercion* (S4); *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem* (S3);
  *The bill* (S5).
- **Media:** None reliable; illustrations are all much later imaginings.
- **Source:** Polybius, *Histories* 6.37–38; Appian, *Civil Wars* 1.118; Plutarch,
  *Life of Crassus* 10; Sara Elise Phang, *Roman Military Service* (Cambridge UP,
  2008).

### The sacramentum and the contubernium

- **What happened:** Roman soldiers swore an oath, the *sacramentum*, binding them to
  obey and not desert; under the empire it was renewed annually and sworn to the
  emperor personally. Below the century, the legion's smallest unit was the
  *contubernium* — eight men who shared a tent and a mule, cooked together and were
  punished together. Decorations included the *corona civica*, an oak wreath awarded
  specifically for saving the life of a fellow citizen in battle.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Three layers of mechanism stacked
  deliberately. The oath is a **commitment device with a religious cost** — breaking
  it is sacrilege, not just desertion. The eight-man tent group is **the primary
  group as an organizational unit**, built rather than left to emerge, and it is the
  direct ancestor of the modern section or squad. The *corona civica* is a
  **selective incentive attached to exactly the behavior a line needs** — not
  killing, which individual valor rewards anyway, but not abandoning the man next to
  you.
- **Could serve:** *Olson* (S3), for the selective incentive; *Costly signals and Big
  Gods* (S5), for the oath; *War* (S1).
- **Media:** Surviving military diplomas and oath texts; Vindolanda tablets for
  everyday unit life. Trajan's Column for tent groups on campaign.
- **Source:** Polybius, *Histories* 6.21 and 6.39; Sara Elise Phang, *Roman Military
  Service: Ideologies of Discipline in the Late Republic and Early Principate*
  (Cambridge UP, 2008); Adrian Goldsworthy, *The Complete Roman Army* (Thames &
  Hudson, 2003).

## Rout: the failure mode

### Ardant du Picq on why lines break

- **What happened:** Charles Ardant du Picq was a French colonel who circulated a
  questionnaire to serving officers asking what actually happened in battles they
  had fought in, and drafted a study of combat from the answers and from ancient
  sources. He was mortally wounded near Metz in 1870; the work was published
  posthumously as *Études sur le combat*.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The founding text of the whole subject, and
  it names the mechanism explicitly. Du Picq's argument is that battle is decided
  morally rather than physically — that two lines rarely meet, because one breaks
  first — and that what holds a line is not courage but mutual acquaintance and
  mutual surveillance. His formulation is worth quoting exactly: "Four brave men who
  do not know each other will not dare to attack a lion. Four less brave, but
  knowing each other well, sure of their reliability and consequently of mutual aid,
  will attack resolutely." That is Olson's group-size argument stated as tactics
  sixty years before Olson.
- **Contested:** Du Picq's evidence is officer recollection and his reading of
  Polybius and Caesar, not systematic data. His influence on French doctrine before
  1914 — where the emphasis on moral force fed the cult of the offensive — is a
  standard criticism, and is itself a useful lesson about a correct mechanism
  applied wrongly.
- **Could serve:** *War* (S1); *Olson* (S3); *Repeated interaction and the folk
  theorem* (S3).
- **Media:** *Battle Studies* is public domain and on Project Gutenberg. No film.
- **Source:** Charles Ardant du Picq, *Études sur le combat* (1880), translated as
  *Battle Studies* (Macmillan, 1921), available via Project Gutenberg.

### Where the casualties actually fall

- **What happened:** Peter Krentz collected the casualty figures given by ancient
  sources for classical Greek hoplite battles between Plataea (479 BC) and Leuctra
  (371 BC). The winning side averaged around 5% dead; the losing side around 14%.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The number is the argument. If standing and
  fighting were what killed people, the two figures would be closer together. The
  gap exists because most of the dying happens *after* one line breaks, when men who
  have turned their backs are run down by men who have not. The individually rational
  move — run — is the one that gets you killed, and the collectively rational move —
  stand — is the one that keeps casualties at 5%. That is the payoff matrix of a
  fighting line, with real numbers in it, and it is the single most useful quantitative
  fact in this whole list.
- **Contested:** Ancient casualty figures are reported by partisan sources and are
  often round numbers; Krentz is explicit that the figures are indicative rather
  than precise. The ratio, not the absolute levels, is the robust part.
- **Could serve:** *The games* (S2) — build the payoff matrix from it; *War* (S1);
  *Introduction to collective action* (S1).
- **Media:** None. This is a table, and it should be shown as one.
- **Source:** Peter Krentz, "Casualties in Hoplite Battles", *Greek, Roman and
  Byzantine Studies* 26 (1985), 13–20.

### Caporetto, October 1917

- **What happened:** On 24 October 1917 German and Austro-Hungarian forces attacked
  the Italian line on the Isonzo using infiltration tactics, gas and concentrated
  artillery. The Italian front collapsed and the army fell back more than 100km to
  the Piave over about two weeks. Italian losses are usually given as around 10,000
  dead and 30,000 wounded, against roughly 265,000 taken prisoner, with a further
  very large number of men separated from their units or dispersed.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The clearest modern illustration of the
  asymmetry du Picq describes, at industrial scale. The ratio of prisoners to dead
  is about 6 to 1, which is the signature of a cohesion collapse rather than a
  defeat by firepower — men stopped being a unit before they stopped being alive.
  Worth teaching alongside the Krentz figures as the same phenomenon two thousand
  years apart. The Italian command's response — Cadorna's public attribution of the
  collapse to the soldiers' own unwillingness, and a documented use of summary
  executions and decimation-style punishment in the Italian army — also makes it a
  test case for whether coercion restores cohesion or destroys it.
- **Contested:** How much of the surrender was demoralization and how much was simple
  encirclement is argued; Cadorna's version was self-serving and was rejected by the
  subsequent commission of inquiry. The scale of Italian decimation practice is
  debated.
- **Could serve:** *War* (S1); *Coercion* (S4), as a case against it.
- **Media:** Extensive photography of the prisoner columns. Ernest Hemingway, *A
  Farewell to Arms* (1929), has the best-known fictional retreat sequence; Mario
  Monicelli's *La Grande Guerra* (1959) is the Italian film treatment.
- **Source:** Mark Thompson, *The White War: Life and Death on the Italian Front
  1915–1919* (Faber, 2008); Vanda Wilcox, *Morale and the Italian Army during the
  First World War* (Cambridge UP, 2016).

## The modern small-unit literature

### Shils and Janowitz on primary groups in the Wehrmacht

- **What happened:** Edward Shils and Morris Janowitz, both working in the
  Psychological Warfare Division of SHAEF, interviewed German prisoners and analyzed
  captured material to explain why the Wehrmacht kept fighting after the war was
  obviously lost. Their 1948 paper argued that the decisive factor was not Nazi
  ideology but the small face-to-face group — the squad and the section — and that
  units disintegrated when those groups were broken up.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The founding modern statement of the
  mechanism, and the reason "primary group cohesion" entered the language. The claim
  is that men fight for roughly ten people they know, that the ideological content of
  the war is largely irrelevant at that scale, and that army-level cohesion is
  therefore a property of thousands of small groups rather than of the army. Note
  what this predicts: destroy the small groups — by casualties, by rotation, by
  replacing men individually — and the army stops working regardless of morale
  campaigns.
- **Contested:** See the next entry. The evidence base is prisoner interviews
  conducted by the enemy at the end of a lost war, which has obvious problems, and
  respondents had reason to attribute their service to comradeship rather than to
  ideology.
- **Could serve:** *Olson* (S3); *War* (S1); *Repeated interaction and the folk
  theorem* (S3).
- **Media:** None. The paper is short and is the material.
- **Source:** Edward A. Shils and Morris Janowitz, "Cohesion and Disintegration in
  the Wehrmacht in World War II", *Public Opinion Quarterly* 12 (1948), 280–315.

### Bartov's counter-argument: ideology and terror

- **What happened:** Omer Bartov argued against the Shils–Janowitz reading on the
  Eastern Front specifically. His case is that casualty rates there were so high that
  primary groups could not survive — units were destroyed and rebuilt repeatedly, so
  there was no stable set of ten men to fight for — and that what held the army
  together instead was a combination of ideological indoctrination and an extremely
  harsh military justice system.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The direct challenge to the course's
  preferred mechanism, and it should be put in explicitly rather than avoided. If
  Bartov is right, cohesion under extreme attrition is produced by **coercion plus
  shared belief** rather than by repeated interaction, because repeated interaction is
  not available. The numbers support the coercion half: estimates of German soldiers
  executed by their own military justice system in the Second World War run to the
  order of 15,000, against one American soldier executed for desertion and none in
  the British forces, which had abolished the death penalty for desertion in 1930.
  The figures come from Manfred Messerschmidt's work on Wehrmacht military law and
  vary between studies.
- **Contested:** The relative weight of ideology, terror and primary groups is not
  settled, and Bartov's argument is strongest for the Eastern Front and weaker
  elsewhere. Treat it as the live alternative hypothesis.
- **Could serve:** *Coercion* (S4); *War* (S1); *The bill* (S5).
- **Media:** Extensive; use with care given the subject matter.
- **Source:** Omer Bartov, *The Eastern Front, 1941–45: German Troops and the
  Barbarisation of Warfare* (Macmillan, 1985) and *Hitler's Army* (Oxford UP, 1991);
  Manfred Messerschmidt, *Die Wehrmachtjustiz 1933–1945* (2005).

### S.L.A. Marshall's ratio of fire — discredited

- **What happened:** In *Men Against Fire* (1947), Brigadier General S.L.A. Marshall
  claimed that only 15–25% of American infantrymen fired their weapons in combat, and
  said the figure came from immediate post-combat group interviews with some 400
  rifle companies. In 1988 Roger Spiller, of the US Army's Combat Studies Institute,
  examined Marshall's notebooks and papers and concluded that the systematic data
  collection Marshall described did not happen — the company figure drifted between
  400, 603 and "something over 500" in Marshall's own later accounts, and the fire
  ratio question does not appear in his interview notes. Spiller's conclusion: "the
  systematic collection of data that made Marshall's ratio of fire so authoritative
  appears to have been an invention."
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Include it, but as the thing that is wrong.
  It was for fifty years the most cited claim about combat behavior, it drove real
  changes in US Army marksmanship training, and it is not supported. Robert Engen's
  later work on Canadian combat reports found nothing resembling Marshall's ratio.
  If the course wants a case about how a striking mechanism story propagates faster
  than the evidence for it, this is the best available one, and it is in our own
  subject area.
- **Contested:** Not really — the methodology critique is broadly accepted. Some
  argue Marshall's underlying qualitative point about reluctance to fire had
  something to it even if the number was fabricated, and that is a defensible but
  much weaker claim.
- **Could serve:** *War* (S1); as a methodological aside anywhere the course cites a
  striking number.
- **Media:** Spiller's article is freely available online. Marshall's book remains in
  print, which is part of the point.
- **Source:** S.L.A. Marshall, *Men Against Fire* (William Morrow, 1947); Roger J.
  Spiller, "S.L.A. Marshall and the Ratio of Fire", *RUSI Journal* 133:4 (1988),
  63–71; Robert Engen, "S.L.A. Marshall and the Ratio of Fire: History, Interpretation,
  and the Canadian Experience", *Canadian Military History* 20 (2011).

### Grossman, *On Killing*

- **What happened:** Dave Grossman's *On Killing* (1995) argues that humans have a
  deep innate resistance to killing their own species, that armies have progressively
  overcome it through conditioning-based training, and that this carries
  psychological costs. The book was widely read inside and outside the military and
  is the main route by which Marshall's figure reached a general audience.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Include it chiefly to be honest about the
  chain of citation. Grossman's central empirical support is Marshall's fire ratio,
  so the critique above propagates directly into it. The thesis is not thereby
  disproved, but it is much less well evidenced than its popularity suggests, and
  parts of Grossman's later work on media violence have been criticized separately.
  If the course wants the "we are reluctant to kill" claim — which would be a
  striking counterweight to the arms-race framing in Session 1 — it needs a better
  source than this one.
- **Contested:** Yes, on the evidence base. Say so if the book comes up, which it
  will, because it is widely read.
- **Could serve:** *War* (S1), with the caveat attached; *The cooperative ape* (S3),
  if a defensible version of the claim can be sourced.
- **Media:** Grossman lectures widely and video is plentiful.
- **Source:** Dave Grossman, *On Killing* (Little, Brown, 1995); for the critique,
  Spiller (1988) and Engen (2011) above.

### Task cohesion versus social cohesion

- **What happened:** A body of later work questions whether interpersonal bonding is
  what produces combat effectiveness at all. Robert MacCoun's 1993 RAND review
  distinguished social cohesion — liking and attachment among members — from task
  cohesion, the shared commitment to doing the job, and found the performance
  evidence supported task cohesion. Anthony King argued from infantry drill and
  training that cohesion is produced by collective competence and rehearsed
  procedure rather than affection. Wong, Kolditz, Millen and Potter's 2003 interviews
  with US soldiers in Iraq found both — trust in comrades, and also trust in leaders'
  competence and stated ideological reasons.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The mechanism dispute that matters most for
  transferring any of this out of the military context. If cohesion runs on **social
  bonds**, it needs stable membership, time and small numbers, and it does not scale.
  If it runs on **task cohesion** — everybody knows the drill, everybody can see
  everybody else executing it, competence is observable — then it is portable, can be
  trained, and survives turnover. That is directly the course's scale question:
  Session 3's mechanisms all need repetition and acquaintance, and task cohesion is a
  candidate for something that works without them.
- **Contested:** Live and unresolved; King and Guy Siebold conducted a sustained
  exchange over it. The policy stakes were real — the social-cohesion argument was
  used in debates over integrating women and openly gay personnel, and MacCoun's work
  was written into that context.
- **Could serve:** *Why this gets harder at scale* (S3); *Olson* (S3); *Cooperation
  among strangers* framing generally (S4).
- **Media:** None specifically.
- **Source:** Robert J. MacCoun, "What Is Known about Unit Cohesion and Military
  Performance", in RAND, *Sexual Orientation and U.S. Military Personnel Policy*
  (1993); Anthony King, *The Combat Soldier* (Oxford UP, 2013); Leonard Wong et al.,
  *Why They Fight: Combat Motivation in the Iraq War* (Strategic Studies Institute,
  2003).

### Replacement systems: van Creveld's comparison

- **What happened:** Martin van Creveld compared German and American army
  organization in the Second World War. Among the differences he emphasized: the
  German system recruited regionally and rebuilt units by withdrawing them and
  refilling them from their own depot, keeping men together, while the US Army used
  an individual replacement system that fed men forward one at a time into units that
  stayed in the line.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A natural experiment on the Shils–Janowitz
  mechanism, run as policy. If cohesion is a property of stable small groups, then an
  individual replacement pipeline is a machine for destroying it — the new man knows
  nobody, nobody knows him, and casualty rates among such replacements were
  notoriously high. The mechanism named here is **administrative: who arrives, with
  whom, and when**. It is also the most directly transferable lesson in this section,
  because it is about organizational design rather than about combat.
- **Contested:** Van Creveld's book is an argument with a thesis and has been
  criticized for admiring the Wehrmacht's professional qualities while underweighting
  its political character and its criminality. Other factors — German defensive
  posture, American material superiority — complicate the performance comparison.
- **Could serve:** *Why this gets harder at scale* (S3); *Olson* (S3); *Changing the
  payoffs* (S4), as institutional design.
- **Source:** Martin van Creveld, *Fighting Power: German and U.S. Army Performance,
  1939–1945* (Greenwood, 1982).

## Mutiny: the collective action problem of the mutineers

### The French army mutinies, 1917

- **What happened:** After the failure of the Nivelle offensive on the Chemin des
  Dames in April 1917, large-scale refusals of duty spread through the French army
  from late April into June. Guy Pedroncini's 1967 archival study found 49 infantry
  divisions affected — nine gravely, fifteen seriously, twenty-five in isolated
  repeated incidents. He counted 3,427 men tried by court-martial, 554 death
  sentences and 49 executions. Pétain, appointed commander-in-chief in May, combined
  selective punishment with leave reform, better food, and a halt to offensives.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The cleanest case for the double structure,
  and the reason mutiny belongs in this course at all. Mutiny is a public good for
  the soldiers — everyone gains if the attacks stop — and the first man to refuse is
  shot, so the individual incentive is to let someone else start. The observed
  mechanism is **threshold and simultaneity**: refusals took the form of units
  declining to move up while agreeing to hold the line, which is a demand
  deliberately shaped to be defensible and to make punishment politically awkward,
  and they spread by contagion between units at rest, where men from different
  divisions met. The suppression mechanism is the mirror image: try a small number,
  publicly, so that the cost falls on identifiable ringleaders rather than on the
  many.
- **Contested:** Figures vary between studies, and the 49 executions figure is
  Pedroncini's; other counts are lower. The word "mutinies" itself is contested — the
  refusals were mostly not insurrectionary, and French historians describe them as a
  strike more than a revolt.
- **Could serve:** *War* (S1); *Olson* (S3); *The reframe* (S2) — the same structure
  read from the mutineers' side rather than the army's.
- **Media:** Stanley Kubrick's *Paths of Glory* (1957) is set in 1916 and is fiction,
  but dramatizes the exemplary-execution mechanism precisely; it was effectively
  unshown in France for decades. *Un long dimanche de fiançailles* (Jeunet, 2004)
  also touches it.
- **Source:** Guy Pedroncini, *Les mutineries de 1917* (PUF, 1967); Leonard V. Smith,
  *Between Mutiny and Obedience* (Princeton UP, 1994), which reads the 5th Infantry
  Division's mutinies as negotiation.

### The Russian army in 1917 and Order No. 1

- **What happened:** On 1 March 1917 the Petrograd Soviet issued Order No. 1,
  directing that elected soldiers' committees be formed in every unit, that weapons
  be under committee control, that deferential forms of address to officers be
  abolished, and that orders from the Provisional Government be obeyed only where
  they did not contradict the Soviet. Discipline in the army deteriorated through the
  year; desertion ran at very large scale, particularly after the failure of the June
  offensive.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The mutineers' coordination problem solved
  from the outside, by a document. A scattered army cannot mutiny because no unit
  knows whether others will act; Order No. 1 supplied **common knowledge and a
  standing institution in one move** — every unit learned simultaneously that every
  other unit was to form a committee, which made refusal safe by making it general.
  It is the best historical illustration in the course of a focal announcement
  flipping an equilibrium, and it works in the direction of dissolving an army rather
  than building one, which makes it a useful counterweight to the rest of this list.
- **Contested:** How far Order No. 1 caused the army's disintegration, as opposed to
  formalizing a collapse already underway, is a long-running historiographical
  argument. Soviet-era and émigré accounts differ sharply; treat monocausal versions
  with suspicion. Desertion figures for 1917 are unreliable.
- **Could serve:** *Schelling* (S4) — focal points and common knowledge; *War* (S1);
  *Coercion* (S4).
- **Media:** The text of Order No. 1 is short and translated; it reads well aloud.
  Extensive 1917 newsreel and photography.
- **Source:** Petrograd Soviet, Order No. 1 (1 March 1917), translated text widely
  available; Allan K. Wildman, *The End of the Russian Imperial Army*, 2 vols
  (Princeton UP, 1980, 1987).

### Spithead and the Nore, 1797

- **What happened:** In April and May 1797 the Channel Fleet at Spithead refused to
  sail, demanding a pay rise, better provisions and the removal of specific abusive
  officers, while undertaking to put to sea if the French came out. The Admiralty
  conceded, around fifty officers were removed, and a royal pardon was granted. A
  second mutiny at the Nore from May into June, under Richard Parker as "President of
  the Delegates of the Fleet", added further demands, attempted to blockade the
  Thames, collapsed as ships defected back one by one, and ended with Parker and some
  twenty-nine others hanged.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Two mutinies a month apart with near-identical
  starting conditions and opposite outcomes, which makes it as close to a controlled
  comparison as this material gets. Spithead held because the demands were narrow and
  shared across every ship, the fleet's delegates met and coordinated in advance, and
  the crews publicly guaranteed to fight the French — a **credible commitment that
  limited the scope of the action and kept the participants defensible**. The Nore
  failed because demands expanded beyond what all crews had a common interest in, so
  the coalition's incentive-compatibility broke and individual ships found it worth
  slipping away. Defection from a mutiny is itself a collective action problem, and
  the Nore is what its unravelling looks like.
- **Could serve:** *Olson* (S3); *Schelling* (S4), on credible commitment; *War* (S1).
- **Media:** Royal Museums Greenwich holds the records and has a research guide; the
  delegates' printed demands survive. No reliable film treatment.
- **Source:** Royal Museums Greenwich, Research Guide B8, "The Spithead and Nore
  mutinies of 1797"; N. A. M. Rodger, *The Command of the Ocean* (Allen Lane, 2004).

### Kiel, 1918, and Invergordon, 1931

- **What happened:** On 29–30 October 1918 crews of the German High Seas Fleet at
  Wilhelmshaven refused to raise steam for a sortie against the Grand Fleet ordered by
  the naval command without government sanction; the refusal spread to Kiel on 3
  November, became a sailors' and workers' rising, and fed directly into the German
  revolution and the armistice. On 15–16 September 1931 around a thousand ratings of
  the British Atlantic Fleet at Invergordon refused duty over pay cuts of up to 25%
  for the longest-serving men; four capital ships could not sail. The cuts were
  reduced, and the resulting run on sterling contributed to Britain leaving the gold
  standard on 21 September.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Ships are the natural experiment for mutiny
  because a crew is a closed, permanently co-present group — high observability, no
  exit, forced repetition — which is exactly the condition list for sustained
  cooperation. Both cases show the same mechanism: **refusal expressed as collective
  inaction rather than violence**, which lowers the individual penalty for any single
  participant and makes it impossible to identify a first mover. At Invergordon the
  ratings organized in the canteen, cheered rather than issued orders, and committed
  no violence, which left the Admiralty with no ringleaders to try. Contrast with the
  Nore, where naming a president gave the state a target.
- **Contested:** The causal weight of Invergordon in the sterling crisis is often
  overstated; it was one pressure among several. Kiel's relationship to the wider
  German revolution is a large literature in itself.
- **Could serve:** *Olson* (S3); *War* (S1); *The reframe* (S2).
- **Media:** Kiel is well photographed and is covered in the German revolution
  literature; the Kiel mutiny memorial exists. Invergordon: Admiralty records and
  contemporary press.
- **Source:** For Kiel, Daniel Horn, *The German Naval Mutinies of World War I*
  (Rutgers UP, 1969); for Invergordon, Anthony Carew, *The Lower Deck of the Royal
  Navy 1900–39* (Manchester UP, 1981), and Alan Ereira, *The Invergordon Mutiny*
  (1981).

## Conscription, free riding and who fights

### Substitution and commutation in the American Civil War

- **What happened:** The Union Enrollment Act of March 1863 made men aged 20–45
  liable to conscription but allowed a drafted man either to furnish a substitute or
  to pay a $300 commutation fee and be excused from that draft call. In the 1863
  draft, of 88,171 men held to service, over 52,000 paid commutation and around
  26,000 provided substitutes; fewer than 10,000 of those drawn actually entered the
  army. Commutation was repealed in July 1864. The Confederacy ran a parallel system
  of substitution and exemptions, including the 1862 provision exempting one white
  man on plantations with twenty or more slaves. "A rich man's war and a poor man's
  fight" was the contemporary slogan, and the New York draft riots of July 1863
  followed the first drawing.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A state that has identified a public good and
  then legislated a price for opting out of contributing to it. The numbers are the
  entry's value: given a legal exit at $300, most of those eligible took it, and the
  share who simply served was under 12% of those held. Free riding is not a moral
  failure here — it is a checkbox on a form, which is exactly the course's point that
  defection needs no villain. It also shows the second-order cost: a buyout priced in
  money converts a shared obligation into a class sorting mechanism, and the
  legitimacy damage was severe enough to force repeal within sixteen months.
- **Contested:** Historians disagree on how unequal the burden actually was —
  estimates of the wealth profile of substitutes and of who paid commutation vary,
  and some work argues the "rich man's war" claim is overstated for the Union as a
  whole.
- **Could serve:** *The games* (S2) — free riding, with real numbers; *Coercion* (S4);
  *Changing the payoffs* (S4), as a case where pricing the obligation broke it.
- **Media:** Draft wheels survive in museum collections (the Philadelphia draft drum
  is one). Extensive Harper's Weekly illustration of the draft and the riots.
- **Source:** Enrollment Act, 12 Stat. 731 (3 March 1863); James W. Geary, *We Need
  Men: The Union Draft in the Civil War* (Northern Illinois UP, 1991); Iver Bernstein,
  *The New York City Draft Riots* (Oxford UP, 1990).

### Mercenary versus citizen armies: Machiavelli and the condottieri

- **What happened:** In *The Prince* (ch. 12–13) and the *Art of War*, Machiavelli
  argued that mercenaries are "useless and dangerous" — disunited, ambitious,
  undisciplined, brave among friends and cowardly against enemies — and that a
  prince must have his own citizen troops. He cites Francesco Sforza, a condottiere
  who took Milan from his employers in 1450. Machiavelli organized a Florentine
  citizen militia in 1506; it was routed at Prato in 1512.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A named historical argument about exactly the
  mechanism this section is about — whether cohesion can be bought or has to be
  grown — and one where the evidence goes largely against the famous version. The
  mechanism Machiavelli is pointing at is real: a wage is a private payment, so a
  mercenary's interest in surviving to collect it runs against the line's need for him
  to stay, while a citizen's stake in the outcome is inalienable. But the historical
  record does not support the conclusion. Michael Mallett's work shows the Italian
  condottieri as long-service professionals under increasingly durable contracts, in
  what became some of Europe's first standing armies, and the "bloodless battle" trope
  Machiavelli relied on is largely his own polemic. Swiss mercenary infantry in the
  same period were famous for holding when broken formations elsewhere did not.
  Machiavelli's own militia losing at Prato is the awkward fact.
- **Contested:** Very. Machiavelli is a participant in the dispute, not a witness to
  it, and he was writing to advocate a policy he had implemented. Teach it as a claim
  and its rebuttal, not as a finding.
- **Could serve:** *War* (S1); *Coercion* (S4); *Changing the payoffs* (S4), on the
  limits of paying for a good rather than organizing it.
- **Media:** *The Prince* ch. 12 is short and in the public domain. Uccello's *Battle
  of San Romano* panels (National Gallery, Uffizi, Louvre) depict condottieri warfare
  and are excellent slide material.
- **Source:** Niccolò Machiavelli, *The Prince* (1532), ch. 12–13, and *Dell'arte
  della guerra* (1521); Michael Mallett, *Mercenaries and their Masters: Warfare in
  Renaissance Italy* (Bodley Head, 1974; repr. with William Caferro, 2009); William
  Caferro, *John Hawkwood* (Johns Hopkins UP, 2006).

## Gaps worth filling

Noted so the next pass doesn't rediscover them:

- The Ottoman janissaries — a slave-soldier corps with barracks living, celibacy
  rules and a shared kitchen as its symbolic center, which then became a political
  veto player. Cohesion engineering that outlived its purpose.
- The Swiss pike squares and the Landsknechte, where the mercenary-versus-citizen
  comparison could be tested properly rather than asserted.
- Israeli and Finnish reserve systems, where the unit is the same group of men for
  decades — a modern deliberate test of the repetition mechanism.
- Insurgent and irregular cohesion — why some guerrilla groups hold under far worse
  conditions than regular armies. Jeremy Weinstein's *Inside Rebellion* (2007) is the
  obvious starting point and would connect to the selective-incentives module.
- Naval press-ganged crews: how the Royal Navy got cohesion out of men taken by
  force, which is an unusually hard case for every mechanism in this list.
