# Fiction and film — candidate list

Candidates, not selections. The premise of this list is that a collective action
problem has to be felt before the formalism means anything, and narrative does
that faster than a treaty summary. Four minutes of a film can put a room inside
a dilemma that a payoff matrix only describes afterwards.

The benchmark is *If a Tree Falls*, already set as prep viewing for Session 1:
people who did something genuinely hard together for years, then informed on
each other within weeks. Not argued for again here. What a candidate has to
match is that quality — the structure visible in the behavior, with no villain
required.

Organised by structure rather than genre, because the structures are what the
course teaches. Coverage is deliberately wide on the interrogation, holdout,
strike and coordination families, thinner on commons-as-plot, which fiction
handles less often than one would expect.

**The standalone question is the operative one.** A 90-minute session can afford
perhaps two clips. An entry that needs ten minutes of plot setup before the
structure is legible is not usable, however good the work is, and that is said
plainly in each entry.

Every film, book, year and director here has been checked. Where a reading is
contested or the fit is weak, the entry says so.

## Betrayal under interrogation

### Goodfellas (1990, Martin Scorsese)

- **The story, briefly:** Henry Hill rises through a New York crew from boyhood
  to middle-aged cocaine dealer, and the film tracks the slow degradation of the
  rules the crew lives by. Arrested and facing a long sentence with his associates
  already killing witnesses, he becomes a government informant and testifies
  against the men he grew up with. He ends in witness protection, complaining
  about the food.
- **What structure it instantiates:** The prisoner's dilemma in its original
  costume, with the detail the textbook leaves out — the payoff structure
  changes before the arrest does. Omertà is a cooperative equilibrium sustained
  by the certainty of retaliation and the expectation of a continuing
  relationship. Once Jimmy starts killing the people who might talk, the future
  payoff from silence goes to zero and the dominant strategy flips. The bite is
  not the interrogation room. It is the arithmetic Henry does beforehand.
- **The scene:** The final stretch, after the Lufthansa heist bodies start
  appearing and Henry is arrested. Two candidates: the sequence where Henry
  realizes Jimmy is arranging his death, and the courtroom testimony that
  follows, in which he addresses the camera directly. Roughly 5-7 minutes if
  taken together, less if just the testimony.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** Partly. The testimony works cold as an image of
  defection, but the *reason* he defects — the collapse of the enforcement
  mechanism that held silence in place — is carried by the preceding twenty
  minutes. Showing it without setup gets you "gangster turns rat", which is the
  uninteresting reading. Two sentences of framing fixes it.
- **Could serve:** Session 2, *The games* (prisoner's dilemma proper). Session 3,
  *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem* — this is the folk theorem failing,
  and the mechanism of its failure is visible.
- **Source:** the film. Adapted from Nicholas Pileggi, *Wiseguy* (1985).

### The Battle of Algiers (1966, Gillo Pontecorvo)

- **The story, briefly:** The FLN builds an urban insurgent network in the Casbah
  of Algiers between 1954 and 1957; French paratroopers under Colonel Mathieu
  dismantle it. The film follows both the construction of the network and its
  systematic unravelling under interrogation, with neither side given a
  sentimental pass.
- **What structure it instantiates:** Two structures, and they are separable.
  First, **organisational design against defection**: the FLN cell is a pyramid
  in which each member knows only three others, which is an explicit engineering
  response to the certainty that anyone can be broken. It is a mechanism for
  limiting the damage a defector can do rather than preventing defection — an
  unusually clear case of designing around a game you know you will lose
  locally. Second, **information extraction as forced defection**: Mathieu's
  method works precisely because it changes each individual's payoff without
  needing to change anyone's loyalty.
- **The scene:** The explanation of the cell structure, with the pyramid drawn
  out, comes in the first third and runs about 3 minutes. Mathieu's press
  conference, in which he lays out the logic of interrogation to journalists in
  flat administrative language, comes in the latter half and runs 4-5 minutes.
  The pyramid scene is the more useful one for this course.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** The pyramid sequence, yes — completely. It is
  diagrammatic, almost lecture-like, and the structure is on screen literally.
  The press conference needs more context to land.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem* (what
  you build when you cannot rely on observation). Session 4, *Coercion*. Session
  1, *War*.
- **Source:** the film.

### Reservoir Dogs (1992, Quentin Tarantino)

- **The story, briefly:** Six criminals who have never met are assembled for a
  diamond robbery by an organiser who forbids them to learn each other's real
  names. The heist goes wrong, the survivors regroup in a warehouse, and the
  film is mostly the argument about which of them informed.
- **What structure it instantiates:** Cooperation without a contract, under
  deliberately engineered anonymity. The colour-coded aliases are the same
  design move as the FLN pyramid — limiting what a defector can give away — but
  here they also destroy the thing that would have made cooperation stable,
  which is reputation. No repeated play, no shared history, no way to verify
  anyone: the diagnostic answers "no" to questions 2, 3 and 4 simultaneously.
  The warehouse argument is what a group does when it has no instrument for
  detecting defection and knows it.
- **The scene:** The warehouse confrontation as the suspicion hardens, in the
  middle third, roughly 5 minutes. The earlier scene in which the organiser
  hands out the aliases and refuses to let them exchange information is shorter,
  about 3 minutes, and is the cleaner teaching object.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** The alias scene, yes. The warehouse argument,
  no — it depends on knowing who actually informed, and the film withholds and
  then reveals that deliberately. Considerable violence in both; worth flagging
  to a group.
- **Could serve:** Session 2, *The games*. Session 3, *Repeated interaction and
  the folk theorem* (as the negative case — what removing repetition does).
- **Source:** the film.

### The Dark Knight (2008, Christopher Nolan)

- **The story, briefly:** The Joker rigs two evacuating ferries with explosives,
  one carrying civilians and one carrying prisoners, and gives each boat the
  detonator for the other. If neither detonates by midnight he destroys both. The
  passengers on each boat debate, vote, and in the end neither pushes the button.
- **What structure it instantiates:** A one-shot two-player prisoner's dilemma
  with the payoff matrix stated out loud by the antagonist, which is rare and
  useful. It is worth being precise that the structure is not *quite* a
  prisoner's dilemma — defection here is not merely self-interested, it is
  lethal to the other party, so the payoffs are closer to a mutual-deterrence
  game with a deadline. The genuinely interesting parts are the ones nobody
  notices: the civilian boat holds a vote and then cannot bring itself to
  execute the vote's outcome, and on the prisoner boat one man resolves the
  problem by removing the option. That is a commitment device, and it is the
  Schelling move.
- **The scene:** The ferry sequence, in the final act, intercut with other
  action. Roughly 6 minutes of screen time for the ferry material, but it is
  spread across a longer stretch and needs either careful editing or accepting
  the intercuts.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** Yes, better than almost anything else on this
  list. The premise is explained inside the scene by the Joker, the stakes are
  visible, and nothing earlier in the film is required. The most reliable cold
  open available.
- **Could serve:** Session 1, *Introduction to collective action*. Session 2,
  *The games* and *The reframe*. Session 4, *Schelling* (the destroyed
  detonator).
- **Source:** the film.

## The holdout, and the group that will not act

### Twelve Angry Men (1957, Sidney Lumet)

- **The story, briefly:** Twelve jurors retire to decide a murder case that
  everyone but one of them regards as obvious. The first ballot is eleven to one
  for conviction. Over 90 minutes in a single hot room, the holdout converts the
  others, one at a time and in a particular order.
- **What structure it instantiates:** Two things at once, and separating them is
  the teaching value. The opening ballot is an **information cascade** —
  several jurors vote guilty because the early voters did, not because of the
  evidence, so private information never enters the room. The conversion is a
  **threshold model** in Granovetter's sense: each juror has a different number
  of defections he needs to see before he switches, and the sequence of
  conversions is ordered by those thresholds rather than by the strength of the
  arguments. The film is, structurally, a demonstration that the equilibrium was
  never stable — it only looked stable because nobody went first.
- **The scene:** The first ballot and the immediate reaction, about 5 minutes in,
  running 4-5 minutes. Or the second ballot, taken by secret vote at the
  holdout's suggestion, where the anonymity is the whole point — a mechanism
  designed to break a cascade. That is roughly a third of the way in and runs
  about 4 minutes.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** Yes. The setup is a jury room, which needs no
  explanation, and the secret-ballot scene in particular carries its own logic
  entirely. The best single-scene candidate on this list for threshold effects.
- **Could serve:** Session 1, *Introduction to collective action*. Session 3,
  *Why this gets harder at scale* (small-N observation and pressure). Session 2,
  *The games* — though note it is not a dilemma, and using it there risks
  blurring the distinction the module exists to protect.
- **Source:** the film. Reginald Rose's teleplay (1954) and stage version are
  substantially the same.

### Antigone (Sophocles, c. 441 BCE)

- **The story, briefly:** Creon forbids the burial of Polynices on pain of death.
  Antigone buries him anyway. Her sister Ismene refuses to help, then tries to
  share the punishment afterwards and is refused. The chorus of Theban elders
  agrees with Antigone throughout and says nothing useful until far too late.
- **What structure it instantiates:** The purest available statement of the
  **volunteer's dilemma with no second volunteer**. Antigone bears the entire
  cost of an act whose benefit — the norm that the dead are buried, and the
  limit on the ruler's writ — is a public good for everyone in Thebes. Ismene's
  refusal is the more instructive half and is usually read as cowardice rather
  than as what it is: an accurate assessment that one additional participant
  does not change the outcome and doubles the losses. The chorus is the
  bystander problem in its oldest form — private agreement, public silence, and
  no mechanism for converting the first into the second.
- **The scene:** The opening dialogue between the two sisters, the first 100
  lines or so, which is the recruitment attempt and the refusal. Roughly 5-8
  minutes in most staged versions. Available in numerous filmed productions and
  audio recordings; the text itself reads aloud well.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** Yes, with one sentence of setup on the burial
  ban. The scene is two people arguing about whether to act, which is
  self-explaining.
- **Could serve:** Session 1, *Introduction to collective action*. Session 3,
  *The cooperative ape* (as counterpoint). Session 5, *The bill* — the norm being
  defended is a kin norm, and Creon's is a civic one, which is the whole
  Henrich argument compressed into a quarrel.
- **Source:** the play. On the political reading, Bernard Williams, *Shame and
  Necessity* (1993), which is where the reading gets contested — the older
  Hegelian framing as a collision of two equally valid claims is not the same
  as the collective action reading, and the difference matters if the text is
  discussed rather than just shown.

### High Noon (1952, Fred Zinnemann)

- **The story, briefly:** A marshal on his wedding day learns that a man he
  convicted is arriving on the noon train with three associates to kill him. He
  spends the intervening 85 minutes — the film runs close to real time — asking
  the townspeople to stand with him. Every one of them finds a reason not to. He
  faces the four alone.
- **What structure it instantiates:** The inverse holdout, and the best example
  in cinema of a **public good that nobody will contribute to because everyone
  reasons correctly about their private return**. Law and order in the town is
  non-excludable; the marshal's survival benefits everyone; and each individual's
  contribution is both risky and, on its own, non-decisive. The refusals are not
  written as cowardice — each is a locally sound argument, which is exactly the
  course's point that defection requires no villain. The church scene is
  Olson's latent group with dialogue.
- **The scene:** The church meeting, roughly the midpoint, running 7-8 minutes. A
  succession of townspeople offer reasons why collective action is somebody
  else's job, including the argument that the marshal leaving would solve the
  problem more cheaply. It can be cut to 4 minutes without losing the structure.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** Yes. A room full of people deciding whether to
  help, with the stakes stated inside the scene. Some setup helps but two
  sentences is enough.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Olson* — the strongest single illustration of the
  latent group available. Session 1, *Introduction to collective action*.
  Session 4, *Coercion* (the state as an institution one person cannot be).
- **Source:** the film.

### An Enemy of the People (Henrik Ibsen, 1882)

- **The story, briefly:** A doctor discovers that the baths his town's new
  prosperity depends on are contaminated by a tannery upstream. He expects to be
  thanked. Instead his brother the mayor, the newspaper, the property owners'
  association and eventually a public meeting all conclude that the finding must
  be suppressed, because the repairs would close the baths for two years and
  ruin everyone. The meeting declares him an enemy of the people.
- **What structure it instantiates:** An externality problem where the polluter
  and the victim are the same town, and the town votes to poison itself. The
  structure is a **collective decision under a shared short-term interest
  against a shared long-term one** — closer to an intertemporal commons than to
  a prisoner's dilemma, since there is no defection to detect, only a
  discounting problem that everyone solves the same way. The mayor's argument is
  worth taking seriously rather than treating as villainy: the costs are certain,
  immediate and local, the benefits diffuse and deferred, and this is the exact
  shape of every failed climate vote since.
- **The scene:** Act IV, the public meeting, which is most of the act — roughly
  20-25 minutes staged. The portion where the mayor lays out the costs and the
  assembly turns can be cut to about 6 minutes.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** With setup, yes: one sentence on the
  contamination and one on the town's dependence on the baths. Without it, no —
  the meeting assumes the audience knows what is being voted on. Many filmed
  productions exist; quality varies considerably.
- **Could serve:** Session 1, *Why this matters so much now*. Session 4,
  *Changing the payoffs* and *Did it ever work?*. Session 3, *Ostrom and the
  commons* (as the failure case against the governed ones).
- **Source:** the play.

## The line that holds and the line that breaks

### Paths of Glory (1957, Stanley Kubrick)

- **The story, briefly:** A French general orders a suicidal attack on a German
  position in 1916. The attack fails; part of the regiment never leaves the
  trench. The general demands that his own artillery fire on the trench, is
  refused, and instead has three men chosen arbitrarily and shot for cowardice
  to restore the others' willingness to advance.
- **What structure it instantiates:** An assault is a threshold public good — it
  succeeds only if enough people go over the top, and the individual who goes
  when others do not simply dies. The men who stay in the trench are at the
  equilibrium. The film's second half is then about **punishment as the
  instrument for shifting that equilibrium**, and specifically about punishment
  that is deliberately arbitrary: the three condemned men are selected by lot and
  by grudge, not by conduct, because what the general needs is not justice but a
  credible expected cost attached to not advancing. This is the coercion module
  with the mask off.
- **The scene:** The attack itself, in the first half, roughly 6 minutes — a
  long lateral tracking shot through the trench and across no man's land, where
  the thinning of the line is visible on screen. The court-martial that follows
  runs about 10 minutes.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** The attack sequence, yes, though it reads as a
  war scene rather than a structure unless framed as threshold. The
  court-martial needs the attack first. Pairing them costs 15 minutes, which is
  too much for a 90-minute session; pick one.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Coercion* — the sharpest available statement of
  what coercion actually is when stripped of legitimacy. Session 1, *War*.
  Session 2, *The games* (threshold goods).
- **Source:** the film. Humphrey Cobb's 1935 novel, and behind it the 1915
  Souain executions.

### Battleship Potemkin (1925, Sergei Eisenstein)

- **The story, briefly:** Sailors on a Russian battleship in 1905 refuse to eat
  maggot-infested meat. The officers order the ringleaders shot; the firing
  squad is assembled, a sailor appeals to it, and the squad lowers its rifles.
  The mutiny succeeds and spreads to the port of Odessa.
- **What structure it instantiates:** The cleanest **threshold cascade** on
  film. Everyone in the firing squad would prefer not to shoot; each of them
  knows that lowering his rifle alone is fatal; and the equilibrium holds until
  one appeal makes the shared preference common knowledge. What changes is not
  anyone's values but their beliefs about everyone else's — which is exactly the
  distinction the course needs between a dilemma, where the payoffs are the
  problem, and a coordination failure, where the beliefs are.
- **The scene:** The tarpaulin and the firing squad, in the second part of the
  film. Roughly 4 minutes. Silent, intertitled, and in the public domain in most
  jurisdictions, which makes it unusually easy to use.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** Yes. The visual grammar does the explaining —
  the covered men, the raised rifles, the hesitation, the lowering. No dialogue
  to follow. It plays cold to an audience that has never heard of 1905. One
  caveat: it is propaganda and should be named as such, though the structure it
  depicts does not depend on the politics.
- **Could serve:** Session 2, *The games* (the assurance/coordination half).
  Session 3, *The cooperative ape*. Session 1, *Introduction to collective
  action*.
- **Source:** the film.

### Spartacus (1960, Stanley Kubrick)

- **The story, briefly:** A slave revolt is defeated. Crassus offers the
  surviving rebels their lives if they identify Spartacus. Spartacus stands;
  then every other man on the field stands and claims the name; Crassus
  crucifies them all.
- **What structure it instantiates:** A **costly signal that works by making
  identification impossible** — each man's claim is individually irrational and
  fatal, and collectively it denies Crassus the thing he wants. It is also a
  clean focal-point case: nobody coordinates, nobody speaks first except
  Spartacus, and the convention propagates instantly because the right move is
  obvious once one person makes it. Two minutes, and it contains both a costly
  signal and a cascade.
- **The scene:** Near the end, after the final battle. Roughly 2 minutes. Among
  the most famous scenes in American cinema, which is both an advantage — most
  of the room will recognise it — and a risk, since familiarity can make people
  stop looking at it.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** Completely. The offer is made inside the scene
  and the response follows immediately. Nothing about the preceding three hours
  is needed.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Schelling* (focal points). Session 5, *Costly
  signals and Big Gods*. Session 2, *The reframe*.
- **Source:** the film. Historically invented; the scene has no basis in Appian
  or Plutarch, and saying so is itself a useful move.

### Xenophon, Anabasis (c. 370 BCE)

- **The story, briefly:** Ten thousand Greek mercenaries are stranded deep in
  Persian territory after the man who hired them is killed at Cunaxa. Their
  generals are then invited to a parley and murdered. Leaderless, surrounded,
  and 1,500 miles from the sea, they hold an assembly overnight, elect new
  commanders, and march out.
- **What structure it instantiates:** An army that loses its command structure in
  a single night should dissolve — each man's best move is to negotiate his own
  terms or slip away, and the collective good of a cohesive march has no
  provider. It does not dissolve. What replaces the generals is an assembly, an
  election, and a set of agreed marching rules, which is as direct a
  demonstration as exists that **institutions are the thing being supplied**,
  not leadership. The recurring problem in the later books — units voting on
  route, plunder and discipline while under attack — is polycentric governance
  under the worst conditions imaginable.
- **The scene:** Book III, chapters 1-2: the night after the generals are killed,
  Xenophon's dream, his address to the surviving officers, and the assembly. A
  reading of 6-8 minutes covers it. There is no adequate film version.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** As a read-aloud passage, yes, with two sentences
  of setup. It is a text rather than a clip, which makes it prep reading or a
  spoken summary rather than a screen moment. Honest assessment: better as a
  short reading circulated before Session 4 than as in-session material.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Polycentricity* and *Coercion*. Session 3, *Olson*
  (a very large group that nonetheless acted — worth asking why, given the
  theory says it should not have).
- **Source:** Xenophon, *Anabasis*. The Rex Warner or Robin Waterfield
  translations read best aloud.

### Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War (c. 400 BCE)

- **The story, briefly:** Two passages. In Book II, plague reaches Athens under
  siege and the observance of law, burial custom and restraint collapses
  together, because the expectation of a future in which reputation matters
  disappears. In Book III, civil war at Corcyra turns every cooperative
  convention — oaths, truces, sanctuary, the meaning of words — into an
  opportunity for whoever moves first.
- **What structure it instantiates:** The **shadow of the future removed, twice,
  by different mechanisms**. In the plague, the discount rate goes to infinity
  because people expect to be dead. At Corcyra, repeated play is destroyed
  because each faction expects the other to defect and moving first is
  decisive — a security dilemma inside a single city. Thucydides' observation
  that the words for virtues changed their meanings is the most precise ancient
  statement of norm collapse, and it maps exactly onto the folk theorem's
  conditions failing.
- **The scene:** Book II, 52-53 (the plague and its effect on conduct) is about
  two pages, 3-4 minutes read aloud. Book III, 82-83 (the Corcyran stasis) is
  roughly three pages, 5 minutes. Either works; the Corcyra passage is the more
  analytically loaded and the harder to follow cold.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** The plague passage does, essentially without
  setup. Corcyra needs a sentence about who is fighting whom, and the prose in
  most translations is dense enough that it should be circulated in writing
  rather than only spoken.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem* (the
  conditions failing). Session 1, *The pattern beneath the metacrisis*.
- **Source:** Thucydides. The Hobbes translation is the most quotable and the
  least accessible; Hammond's for the Oxford World's Classics is the clearest.

## Free riding, strikes and collective bargaining

### Germinal (Émile Zola, 1885)

- **The story, briefly:** Étienne Lantier arrives at a northern French coal
  mining village, is radicalized, and leads a strike against a wage cut. The
  strike holds for months at enormous cost, breaks when hunger and internal
  division set in, and ends with a pit flooded by sabotage and the survivors
  going back on worse terms than they started.
- **What structure it instantiates:** Olson's problem in full, with all its
  parts on the page. A won strike is a public good across the workforce; the
  cost falls entirely on whoever strikes; the provident fund is the selective
  incentive and it is nowhere near large enough; and the men who keep working
  are free riders whose reasoning is given sympathetically rather than
  dismissed. Zola also has the enforcement half — the marching crowd that goes
  pit to pit to shut down the men still working is **peer punishment as the
  substitute for an absent institution**, and the novel is clear that it is
  violent, effective, and the reason the strike held as long as it did.
- **The scene:** The crowd's march across the coalfield in Part V, where the
  strikers move from pit to pit forcing the working men out. Roughly 15 pages.
  Claude Berri's 1993 film adaptation covers it in about 8 minutes and is
  usable. The forest assembly earlier, where the strike is voted, is the better
  short excerpt at 4-5 minutes.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** The forest assembly, with setup on the wage cut,
  yes. The march, less so — without knowing that the men being forced out are
  fellow miners rather than company men, it reads as a riot.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Olson* — the single best literary illustration of
  selective incentives being insufficient. Session 4, *Coercion*. Session 2,
  *The games*.
- **Source:** the novel. Berri's *Germinal* (1993) for the footage.

### Norma Rae (1979, Martin Ritt)

- **The story, briefly:** A textile mill worker in a small Southern town is
  recruited by a union organiser, becomes the visible local face of the drive,
  and is fired for it. The mill wins the vote anyway.
- **What structure it instantiates:** A **threshold problem solved by a visible
  first mover**. The union election requires a majority; every individual who
  signs risks their job; and the film's mechanism for breaking the deadlock is
  making one person's commitment unmissable, which converts a private decision
  into an observable one. The famous scene is a demonstration that in threshold
  problems the binding constraint is often not preference but the inability to
  see what others prefer.
- **The scene:** Near the end: Norma Rae is fired, writes the word UNION on a
  piece of cardboard, stands on a work table and holds it up until the machines
  are switched off one by one down the length of the floor. Roughly 3 minutes,
  with no dialogue in the crucial portion.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** Yes, almost entirely — the machines going quiet
  one at a time is a cascade rendered in sound, and it needs only the
  information that this is a union drive. One of the three best cold-open
  candidates on this list.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Olson*. Session 2, *The reframe* — this is a
  collective action problem being solved, and the course needs successes early.
  Session 1, *Introduction to collective action*.
- **Source:** the film. Based on Crystal Lee Sutton's organising at the J.P.
  Stevens plant in Roanoke Rapids.

### Matewan (1987, John Sayles)

- **The story, briefly:** A union organiser arrives in a West Virginia coal town
  in 1920 where the company has cut wages and imported Black and Italian workers
  to break the strike. He persuades the three groups — local white miners, the
  imported Black miners, the Italian immigrants — that they are one bargaining
  unit rather than three, over the company's determined efforts to keep them
  apart. It ends in the Matewan massacre.
- **What structure it instantiates:** **Coalition formation as the actual
  problem**, which the strike literature usually skips. The company's strategy is
  not to defeat the union but to ensure the relevant group is never defined as
  one group, which is a much cheaper intervention. The structure is closest to a
  stag hunt with an identity problem attached: cooperation pays if everyone
  cooperates, but the boundary of "everyone" is exactly what is in dispute, and
  each faction's belief that the others will hunt rabbit is self-fulfilling.
  Useful precisely because the course's later sessions argue that in-group
  formation is the mechanism of cooperation at scale — here it is being
  manufactured in real time, and the bill for it is visible.
- **The scene:** The scene where the striking miners encounter the imported
  Black workers at the camp, and the organiser argues that both groups are being
  used against each other. Mid-film, roughly 5-6 minutes.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** Needs setup on who has been brought in and why —
  perhaps three sentences. With that, the argument inside the scene is
  self-contained.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Olson*. Session 5, *Multi-level selection* and
  *The bill* — group boundaries being drawn, with the consequences visible.
  Session 4, *Did it ever work?*.
- **Source:** the film. The historical Battle of Matewan, 19 May 1920.

### Lysistrata (Aristophanes, 411 BCE)

- **The story, briefly:** The women of the Greek cities agree to withhold sex
  from their husbands until the men end the Peloponnesian War. They also seize
  the treasury on the Acropolis, which funds it. The agreement nearly collapses
  immediately when several of them invent reasons to go home.
- **What structure it instantiates:** A **cartel**, and Aristophanes is
  unusually rigorous about it. The collective benefit requires universal
  compliance; each individual's defection is privately advantageous and hard for
  the others to observe; so the play's central problem is enforcement, not
  persuasion. The oath scene exists because a promise is insufficient. The scenes
  where women manufacture excuses to leave are free riding written 2,400 years
  ago, and they are funnier and more precise than most modern treatments. The
  seizure of the treasury is the selective incentive — the one thing that gives
  the coalition leverage independent of individual compliance.
- **The scene:** The oath over the wine bowl, early in the play — 4-5 minutes
  staged. Or the sequence of attempted desertions, roughly the middle, which is
  the free-riding material proper and runs about 5 minutes.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** Yes, with a sentence of setup. It is broad
  comedy, translations vary enormously in explicitness, and a group should be
  told what they are getting. Many filmed and audio productions; the text is
  more reliable than most of them.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Olson* (selective incentives, and the monitoring
  problem). Session 2, *The games*. Session 4, *Changing the payoffs*.
- **Source:** the play. Translations differ substantially in tone; Jeffrey
  Henderson's is the standard scholarly one.

## Commons, resources and externalities

### Wolf Totem (Jiang Rong, 2004)

- **The story, briefly:** A Beijing student is sent to the Inner Mongolian
  grassland during the Cultural Revolution and is taught the local ecology by an
  old herdsman. Han settlement policy brings wolf extermination and arable
  farming; the wolves go, gazelle and rat populations change, the grassland is
  ploughed, and within a generation the Olonbulag is turning to dust.
- **What structure it instantiates:** A **subtractable commons destroyed by the
  replacement of one governance regime with another**, which is a more accurate
  description of most real commons failures than "open access". The Mongol
  system has rules — which pastures, when, and an explicit tolerance of wolves
  as the mechanism that keeps grazers moving. The incoming regime has production
  targets. Nobody in the novel is stupid and nobody is greedy in the cartoon
  sense; the failure is that a set of local rules with no formal standing is
  overridden by a planner who cannot see what they were for. That is the Ostrom
  argument told from inside.
- **The scene:** The gazelle hunt on the frozen lake, roughly a third in — the
  herdsmen take part of the wolves' cached kill and deliberately leave the rest,
  and the reasoning for the restraint is stated. About 10 pages. Jean-Jacques
  Annaud's 2015 film covers the equivalent material in about 8 minutes.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** The film version needs setup — without knowing
  the quota policy, the restraint looks like superstition rather than a rule.
  The book passage reads better but is long. Honest assessment: this is prep
  reading or a two-minute told summary, not a cold clip.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Ostrom and the commons* — the strongest non-Western
  narrative case available for local rules doing real work. Session 5,
  *Multi-level selection*. Session 4, *Polycentricity*.
- **Source:** the novel, translated by Howard Goldblatt (2008). Note: the novel's
  broader argument — about national character and the superiority of "wolf"
  cultures — is contested and unpleasant, and is separable from the ecological
  material. Say so if you use it.

### Jaws (1975, Steven Spielberg)

- **The story, briefly:** A shark kills a swimmer at a New England resort town
  days before the Fourth of July. The police chief wants the beaches closed; the
  mayor and the business owners do not, because the town's entire annual income
  arrives in that fortnight. The beaches stay open and more people die.
- **What structure it instantiates:** An externality and a **coordination
  problem with a first-mover penalty**, though the fit is looser than the film's
  reputation suggests. The mayor is not free riding — he is making a
  distributional calculation about certain economic ruin versus probabilistic
  death, and the town's position would be different if any single business could
  close without losing to the others. Worth including because the *argument* is
  so familiar: the costs of precaution are certain, immediate and concentrated,
  the benefits uncertain, deferred and diffuse. That is the climate vote again.
- **The scene:** The town council meeting where the chief's beach closure is
  overruled, early in the film, about 4 minutes. Or the exchange on the beach
  before the Fourth, shorter.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** Yes. Everyone knows what the film is, the
  meeting scene explains its own stakes, and it takes four minutes.
- **Does the reading hold?** Partly. This is a straightforward
  cost-benefit-under-uncertainty problem with a bad decision at the end of it,
  and calling it a collective action problem is a stretch — there is no
  defection and no free riding, just an official weighing concentrated losses
  against diffuse ones. Use it as the *shape* of a climate argument rather than
  as a game.
- **Could serve:** Session 1, *Why this matters so much now*. Session 4,
  *Changing the payoffs*.
- **Source:** the film.

### Things Fall Apart (Chinua Achebe, 1958)

- **The story, briefly:** Okonkwo is a prominent man in an Igbo village in the
  1890s. Missionaries arrive, converts begin to accumulate, and the village's
  ability to enforce any of its own rules erodes — first at the margins, then
  entirely. The novel ends with Okonkwo's suicide and the village unable to act
  even in its own defence.
- **What structure it instantiates:** **Norm enforcement collapsing once exit
  becomes available**. Every rule in the village is sustained by the certainty of
  collective sanction — ostracism, fines, the egwugwu's judgment — and all of
  those depend on there being nowhere else to go. The mission provides an
  outside option, and from that point each individual's cost of violating a norm
  falls, which lowers the cost for the next person, and the equilibrium unwinds.
  Achebe is precise about the sequence: the first converts are people who were
  already marginal and had least to lose, which is exactly the threshold model's
  prediction about who moves first.
- **The scene:** The killing of the sacred python and the village's response, in
  the final third — roughly 6 pages. The village debates its options, finds that
  the usual sanction no longer bites, and settles on an ostracism that the
  converts can simply absorb. Reads aloud in about 6 minutes.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** As a reading, with setup on what the python
  means and who the converts are, yes. There is no good film version. Prep
  reading rather than in-session material.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem*
  (punishment losing its force). Session 5, *Costly signals and Big Gods*, and
  *The bill*. Session 6, *What modernity already did* — the enlargement seen
  from the receiving end.
- **Source:** the novel.

## Trust without verification

### The Treasure of the Sierra Madre (1948, John Huston)

- **The story, briefly:** Three American drifters in 1920s Mexico prospect for
  gold together. They find it. The rest of the film is the three of them
  managing the fact that each now has a large and portable asset, no law within
  reach, and two partners who could take it.
- **What structure it instantiates:** A sustained **trust game with no
  enforcement**, and the film's great move is showing the institutional
  improvisation. They divide the gold into three shares nightly and each man
  hides his own, which converts one large theft into three small ones and makes
  defection observable. They set a watch rota. They discuss, explicitly, what
  happens if one of them is hurt. All of this is Ostrom's design principles
  being invented from scratch by three men who have never heard of them — and
  then failing, because there is no mechanism at all for the case where one
  partner is left alone with the others' shares.
- **The scene:** The scene where they agree on dividing and separately hiding the
  shares, roughly the midpoint, about 4 minutes. The later scene where one of
  them is alone with the full pile is the payoff and comes considerably later.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** The division scene, yes — it is three men
  negotiating a rule and explaining why, which needs no context beyond "they
  found gold". Strong candidate.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Ostrom and the commons* — rules being designed in
  real time, which is more vivid than the design principles as a list. Session 3,
  *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem*. Session 2, *The games*.
- **Source:** the film. B. Traven's 1927 novel.

### Lagaan (2001, Ashutosh Gowariker)

- **The story, briefly:** A drought-stricken village in 1890s central India is
  told by the local British cantonment commander that it can have three years
  free of land tax if it beats his officers at cricket, and will owe triple if
  it loses. One farmer accepts on the village's behalf, without asking them. He
  then has to assemble eleven players from a village that has never seen the
  game, does not want the bet, and is divided by caste.
- **What structure it instantiates:** Recruitment into a costly collective
  enterprise where the downside is shared whether or not you play — the tax
  falls on everyone — and the effort is voluntary. That is the free-rider
  structure exactly, and it runs for most of the film's middle hour. The caste
  material is the more interesting part: the team is short a player, the
  available player is an untouchable, and including him means the existing
  members bear a social cost to gain a collective benefit. **Expanding the
  boundary of the cooperating group has a price, and someone has to pay it
  first.** That is Session 5's argument in a single scene, and the film takes it
  seriously rather than resolving it cheaply.
- **The scene:** The confrontation over including Kachra, the untouchable
  bowler — roughly the midpoint of a very long film (the whole thing runs close
  to four hours). About 6 minutes.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** Needs setup: the wager, and why one more player
  matters. Three sentences. Given that, the scene is a group arguing about who
  counts as one of us, which is legible anywhere.
- **Could serve:** Session 5, *The bill* and *Multi-level selection*. Session 6,
  *Enlarging the sphere of concern*. Session 3, *Olson* (recruitment and
  free riding).
- **Source:** the film.

## Disaster, pandemic and the bystander

### The Plague (Albert Camus, 1947)

- **The story, briefly:** Plague closes the Algerian city of Oran. A doctor, a
  journalist trying to leave, a priest, a clerk and a smuggler each work out what
  they owe the city. Volunteer sanitary squads form and do dangerous, unglamorous
  work for months. Some people join, most do not, and the novel declines to make
  much of the difference.
- **What structure it instantiates:** **Voluntary provision of a public good
  under real personal risk**, treated without heroics — Camus is explicit that
  joining the squads should not be admired excessively, because treating it as
  exceptional implies that not joining is the normal case. Tarrou's organisation
  of the squads is the mechanism: a specific, bounded, visible task that a person
  can be asked to do, which is how latent groups become active ones. Rambert's
  arc is the free rider's, fully argued rather than caricatured — he has a
  defensible case for leaving, and the novel does not defeat it so much as let
  him change his mind.
- **The scene:** Part II, the formation of the sanitary squads and the narrator's
  refusal to over-praise them. Roughly 5 pages, 6-7 minutes read aloud. Rambert's
  argument with Rieux about whether he is entitled to leave is the sharper
  passage and about the same length.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** As a reading, yes. No usable film version — the
  1992 adaptation relocates the story and is not recommended. Prep reading.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape*. Session 1, *Introduction to
  collective action*. Session 6, *Enlarging the sphere of concern*.
- **Source:** the novel.

### Contagion (2011, Steven Soderbergh)

- **The story, briefly:** A novel respiratory virus spreads from Hong Kong
  worldwide. The film follows epidemiologists, a WHO investigator, a bereaved
  husband, and a blogger who promotes a fake cure while shorting the market. A
  vaccine arrives after a year and has to be allocated.
- **What structure it instantiates:** Three distinct structures, which is why it
  is worth a slot despite being uneven. **Free riding on a fear cascade** — the
  blogger's forsythia sells because individuals rationally hedge, and the
  pharmacy run is the result. **Insider defection** — a CDC official warns his
  wife to leave Chicago before the quarantine is announced, which is a small
  private act that destroys the informational public good the announcement
  depends on. And **allocation by focal rule** — the vaccine is distributed by a
  televised lottery on date of birth, which is arbitrary, transparent, immune to
  influence, and therefore acceptable. That last one is a Schelling solution in
  a mainstream film and is not easy to find elsewhere.
- **The scene:** The birthday lottery, in the closing 15 minutes, about 2
  minutes. Or the CDC official's phone call to his wife, roughly two-thirds in,
  about 3 minutes — a man doing something indefensible for entirely
  comprehensible reasons, which is the course's thesis in miniature.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** The lottery, yes, given one sentence. The phone
  call needs to know he is a CDC official under a communications embargo. Both
  are short enough to use together for under 6 minutes.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Schelling* (the lottery as focal point). Session 1,
  *Why this matters so much now*. Session 2, *The games*.
- **Source:** the film.

### Do the Right Thing (1989, Spike Lee)

- **The story, briefly:** One very hot day on one block in Bedford-Stuyvesant. A
  complaint about the photographs on the wall of the pizzeria escalates across
  the day, a police chokehold kills a neighbourhood man, and the crowd, after a
  pause, destroys the pizzeria.
- **What structure it instantiates:** A **threshold model of collective
  behaviour** in Granovetter's sense, and the film is almost diagrammatic about
  it. The crowd holds for a measurable beat after the killing. Different people
  need different numbers of others to move before they move. The person with the
  lowest threshold acts alone, and the rest follows — and the film is careful
  that the first act is not the most enraged person but the one closest to the
  owner, which complicates any reading based on preference intensity. Also a
  bystander case earlier in the day: several people witness the escalation,
  each could defuse it, none does.
- **The scene:** The sequence from the chokehold to the first thrown object,
  final act, roughly 5 minutes. The pause before the crowd moves is the part to
  point at.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** No. It depends on the day the film has built and
  on knowing the relationships between the people in the street, and shown cold
  it is simply a riot. Honest assessment: a strong structure in a clip that does
  not travel. Usable as prep viewing, not as a 4-minute insert.
- **Could serve:** Session 2, *The games* (threshold goods). Session 3, *Why this
  gets harder at scale*.
- **Source:** the film. Mark Granovetter, "Threshold Models of Collective
  Behavior", *AJS* 83:6 (1978), for the model it instantiates.

## Arms races

### Dr. Strangelove or: How I Learned to Stop Worrying and Love the Bomb (1964, Stanley Kubrick)

- **The story, briefly:** A US general orders a nuclear strike on the USSR
  outside the chain of command. In the War Room, the president and the Soviet
  ambassador try to recall the bombers, and the ambassador reveals that the USSR
  has built a doomsday machine which will detonate automatically if the country
  is attacked, and which cannot be switched off. One bomber gets through.
- **What structure it instantiates:** The **commitment device**, and the
  precise reason a commitment device has to be both automatic and announced. The
  doomsday machine is Schelling's argument rendered as farce: deterrence works by
  removing your own ability to choose, since a threat you could decline to
  execute is not credible. The joke that carries the whole theory is that the
  Soviets built it and had not yet announced it, which makes it strategically
  worthless — and Strangelove says so in as many words. The film is also, more
  quietly, about delegation under an arms race: the authority to launch was
  pushed downward precisely because of the fear of a decapitating first strike,
  which is a security dilemma producing the accident it was designed to prevent.
- **The scene:** The War Room sequence where the doomsday machine is revealed and
  explained, roughly two-thirds through. About 5 minutes, self-contained, mostly
  three people at a table talking.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** Yes, with one sentence of setup ("a US general
  has launched an unauthorised strike and they cannot recall it"). The theory is
  explained inside the scene by the characters, which is the rarest property on
  this list. Among the three best cold clips here.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Schelling* — the anchor clip for the module, and
  given the module's importance to the AI audience, probably the single highest-
  value clip in the course. Session 1, *War* and *The pattern beneath the
  metacrisis*.
- **Source:** the film. Thomas Schelling, *The Strategy of Conflict* (1960), for
  the mechanism — the doomsday machine derives from the same discussions
  Schelling was part of, and pairing the two is honest rather than a stretch.
  Sidney Lumet's *Fail Safe* (1964) tells a near-identical story straight, and
  is the better choice for a group that will not take the satire seriously.

## Coordination, and one famous case that does not work

### Seven Samurai (1954, Akira Kurosawa)

- **The story, briefly:** A farming village that expects to be raided after the
  harvest decides to hire masterless samurai, paying in rice it can barely
  spare. Seven are recruited. They fortify the village, train the farmers, and
  fight off forty bandits at heavy cost.
- **What structure it instantiates:** Defence is a threshold public good and the
  film works through its provision problem in order. The village debate is
  whether to act at all; the recruitment is a bargaining problem with nothing to
  offer; the fortification requires abandoning three outlying houses, which the
  three families object to, and Kambei's answer — that the village cannot defend
  everything and those who will not accept the sacrifice will be cut loose — is
  the clearest statement anywhere of why a group defending a perimeter must
  first define the perimeter. The farmers' concealed weapons and hidden food are
  the free-riding subplot: the village has been defecting on its own defenders
  in advance.
- **The scene:** The village council near the opening — whether to fight, flee,
  or appeal to the magistrate, and the old man's decision. Roughly 6 minutes. Or
  the abandonment of the outlying houses, about a third in, roughly 4 minutes,
  which is the sharper structural scene.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** The council scene, yes, with a sentence on the
  bandits. The film runs 3.5 hours, so nobody will have watched it as prep;
  choose a scene that self-explains.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Olson* and *The cooperative ape*. Session 4,
  *Coercion* (hiring violence as a service is a state in embryo). Session 5,
  *War as ratchet*.
- **Source:** the film.

### A Beautiful Mind (2001, Ron Howard)

- **The story, briefly:** A biopic of John Nash. In a bar, Nash watches his
  friends prepare to compete for the attention of one woman and has a flash of
  insight, which the film presents as the origin of his equilibrium concept: if
  they all approach her they block each other, so they should each approach one
  of her friends instead.
- **What structure it instantiates:** Nothing correctly, and that is the reason
  to use it. The strategy profile the scene proposes is not a Nash equilibrium —
  each man has an incentive to deviate and approach the woman while the others
  are occupied elsewhere — and the scene in fact describes a cooperative,
  collectively optimal outcome, which is the thing Nash equilibrium is famously
  *not*. Showing it and asking a room to find the error is a faster route into
  the definition than defining it, and it inoculates against the most widespread
  popular misunderstanding of the concept.
- **The scene:** The bar scene, roughly 30 minutes in. About 3 minutes.
- **Does the clip stand alone?** Completely. It is a self-contained set piece
  with its own explanation built in, which is what makes it such an effective
  wrong answer.
- **Could serve:** Session 2, *Some basic game theory* — as the worked error, not
  as exposition. Nowhere else.
- **Source:** the film. The error is well documented; the clearest short
  treatment is in the game theory teaching literature rather than in film
  criticism, and any standard textbook's definition of equilibrium is sufficient
  to make the point live.

## Notes on coverage

**Thin areas.** Commons collapse as a plot is genuinely rare — fiction prefers a
villain, and a commons failure has none. *Wolf Totem* and *An Enemy of the
People* are the two strong entries and neither is a clean clip. If this is the
structure the course most needs footage for, documentary is likely to serve
better than drama.

**Coordination with no communication** is also thin. *Spartacus* and the
*Potemkin* firing squad are the two clean cases, and both are cascades rather
than pure focal-point problems. The best focal-point material in this file is the
*Contagion* birthday lottery, which arrives in a form nobody expects.

**Interrogation and plea bargaining** is over-supplied by comparison, and the
four entries here could be cut to two without loss. *Goodfellas* and *The Dark
Knight* carry it.

**Non-Anglophone and non-Western:** *Seven Samurai*, *The Battle of Algiers*,
*Lagaan*, *Wolf Totem*, *Things Fall Apart*, *Battleship Potemkin*, *Germinal*,
*The Plague*, plus the three ancient Greek entries. Roughly half the list.
