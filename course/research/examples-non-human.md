# Non-human cooperation — candidate examples

Long-list for the **cooperative ape** module in Session 3, and for the opening
of Session 1 where the point is that cooperation is not a human invention.
Over-generated deliberately; cut later.

Footage is a selection criterion here, not an afterthought. Several of these
cases are chosen over more spectacular ones because three minutes of BBC film
exists. Where nothing filmable exists, the entry says so.

Roughly a third of the entries are cooperation being cheated rather than
cooperation working. That balance is intentional: defection in slime moulds and
bacteria is the cleanest available evidence that free riding is a structural
consequence of the payoff matrix rather than a human moral failing.

Two standing cautions, flagged again in the individual entries:

- Nature documentary narration routinely upgrades coincidence into teamwork.
  Where the literature disputes the cooperative reading, the entry says what the
  dispute is.
- Footage sourced to a BBC Earth YouTube channel clip is a cut from the parent
  series. The clips are 2–5 minutes and directly usable; the full sequences are
  longer and need the disc or the iPlayer archive.

## 1. Musk oxen defensive circle

### The case

- **What happens:** Musk oxen (*Ovibos moschatus*) facing wolves or bears form a
  line or a closed ring, adults outward with horns presented, calves inside.
  Individuals occasionally break out to charge and then rejoin. Against wolves
  the formation is close to impenetrable as long as it holds; herds that break
  and run lose calves.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Defense is a public good produced by
  joint effort, and it only exists above a participation threshold — a half-formed
  ring is worse than none. The individual incentive is to be on the inside.
  What stops defection is mostly that there is nowhere better to be: an animal
  that bolts from the ring is the one that gets taken, so the private and
  collective interest coincide once the formation exists. It is a coordination
  problem more than a dilemma, which makes it a good early contrast with the
  prisoner's dilemma cases in Session 2.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape*. Also Session 2, *The games*,
  as a non-human stag hunt — the payoff structure is assurance, not temptation.
- **Footage:** Good. Musk ox defensive formations against Arctic wolves are
  filmed in BBC *Snow Wolf Family and Me* (2014, Gordon Buchanan) and in Arctic
  wildlife documentary stock generally. The US National Park Service maintains a
  public page on muskox circle defense at Gates of the Arctic with images. Note
  that a full attack sequence is rare film; standing formations are common.
- **Source:** Gray, D.R., *The Muskoxen of Polar Bear Pass* (1987). National
  Park Service, "Muskox: Circle Defense", nps.gov/gaar. Treat popular-web claims
  about precise formation times (e.g. "23–40 seconds") as unsourced — they trace
  to content farms, not to a paper.

## 2. Cape buffalo mobbing predators

### The case

- **What happens:** African buffalo (*Syncerus caffer*) herds turn on lions that
  have taken a member of the herd, and can drive them off and recover the
  victim. The best-known instance is a single amateur video shot at Kruger in
  2007 in which a buffalo calf is taken by lions, dragged partly into water by a
  crocodile, and then recovered alive after the herd returns and mobs the pride.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Mobbing is costly to the individual
  and the benefit is diffuse. The free-rider option — hang back, let others
  charge — is available and taken by many animals in the herd. What makes it
  work is that the cost per participant falls sharply as numbers rise, which is
  the standard mobbing payoff, and a version of Olson's point about threshold
  effects in reverse.
- **Could serve:** Session 1, *Introduction to collective action*, as a first
  case that is memorable and legible without any theory. Session 3, *The
  cooperative ape*.
- **Footage:** Excellent, and unusually well-suited to teaching. "Battle at
  Kruger", filmed by Dave Budzinski and Jason Schlosberg, YouTube, 2007, about 8
  minutes. It is amateur footage — shaky, unnarrated, no score — which is an
  advantage: it is a case, not a story someone has already told you how to feel
  about. There is also a National Geographic documentary built around it (*Caught
  on Safari: Battle at Kruger*, 2008).
- **Source:** The video itself is the primary record. For the behavior generally,
  Prins, H.H.T., *Ecology and Behaviour of the African Buffalo* (1996).

## 3. Meerkat sentinels — and why the standard telling is wrong

### The case

- **What happens:** Foraging meerkats (*Suricata suricatta*) take turns on
  raised lookout, giving alarm calls that send the group to cover. It is the
  textbook example of altruistic vigilance, and the textbook is probably wrong.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** It looks like a rota for a public
  good — costly personal vigilance, benefit shared. Clutton-Brock and colleagues
  found otherwise. Sentinels guard from safe sites, go on guard only once their
  own stomachs are full, take no regular turns, and are typically the first to
  spot and escape a predator. Over 2,000 hours of observation, no raised guard
  was killed. Provisioning animals with extra food *increased* their guarding.
  The conclusion is that guarding is the individually optimal activity for a fed
  animal when nobody else is up, which makes it not much of a collective action
  problem at all.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape* — and this is the entry that
  earns its place by being a correction. Also Session 2, *Some basic game
  theory*, on why establishing that a behavior is genuinely costly is prior to
  calling it altruism. Useful as a methodological warning before the course
  starts making claims about human cooperation.
- **Footage:** Excellent and abundant. *Meerkat Manor* (Animal Planet / Oxford
  Scientific Films, 2005–2008, four series) is built on the Kalahari Meerkat
  Project population. BBC *Planet Earth* and *Africa* both carry sentinel
  sequences. Short BBC Earth YouTube cuts exist.
- **Source:** Clutton-Brock, T.H. et al., "Selfish Sentinels in Cooperative
  Mammals", *Science* 284(5420):1640–1644, 1999.
  https://www.science.org/doi/abs/10.1126/science.284.5420.1640

## 4. Fork-tailed drongos and the false alarm call

### The case

- **What happens:** Fork-tailed drongos (*Dicrurus adsimilis*) in the Kalahari
  follow meerkats and pied babblers and give genuine alarm calls when a raptor
  appears, which the mammals act on. They also give *false* alarm calls when a
  target has dug up something worth eating, and take the abandoned food. When a
  target stops responding to the drongo's own alarm, the drongo switches to
  mimicking the target species' alarm call — including the meerkat sentinel's own
  call, which the meerkats cannot afford to ignore.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** An alarm-call system is a public good
  that runs on the credibility of the signal. The drongo is a free rider who has
  discovered that the good can be converted into private benefit by corrupting
  the signal, and the flexible switching between call types is a direct response
  to the victims' learning — an arms race in miniature. About 55% of drongo
  kleptoparasitism attempts use false alarms, with a 35% success rate. The
  system survives because drongos cannot cheat too often without destroying the
  credibility they depend on, which is precisely the constraint the course keeps
  returning to.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape*, as the best single case for
  "defection is structural". Session 4, *Did it ever work?*, on monitoring and
  the cost of verification.
- **Footage:** Excellent — probably the single best-filmed cheating case
  available. BBC *Africa* (2013), episode 1 "Kalahari", narrated by David
  Attenborough, carries the full sequence: genuine warning, then a false alarm,
  then the meerkats learning to ignore it, then the drongo escalating to mimicry
  of the meerkat's own call. Cut as "Drongo Bird Tricks Meerkats | Africa | BBC
  Earth" on YouTube, roughly 4 minutes.
  https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tEYCjJqr21A
- **Source:** Flower, T.P., "Fork-tailed drongos use deceptive mimicked alarm
  calls to steal food", *Proc. R. Soc. B* 278(1711):1548–1555, 2011. Flower,
  T.P., Gribble, M. & Ridley, A.R., "Deception by Flexible Alarm Mimicry in an
  African Bird", *Science* 344(6183):513–516, 2014.

## 5. Vervet monkey alarm calls

### The case

- **What happens:** Vervets (*Chlorocebus pygerythrus*) give acoustically
  distinct alarm calls for leopards, eagles and snakes, and conspecifics respond
  with the appropriate escape — into trees, look up, look down — even when the
  call is played from a hidden speaker with no predator present. Infants
  over-generalize (eagle alarms for any bird) and get more accurate with age.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Calling is the classic costly public
  good: the caller draws attention to itself and the benefit goes to everyone in
  earshot. The cost is real but smaller than usually assumed, and kin selection
  does much of the explanatory work in a group of relatives. Worth pairing with
  the drongo entry, since a functioning alarm system is exactly what the drongo
  parasitizes.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape*. Pair directly with entry 4.
- **Footage:** Moderate. The Seyfarth/Cheney playback experiments were filmed at
  Amboseli, and archival clips appear in primate behavior teaching material and
  in BBC *Life of Mammals* / *Trials of Life* material on primate communication.
  No single well-known documentary sequence of the calibre of the drongo film.
  Audio of the three call types is easy to source and works well on its own.
- **Source:** Seyfarth, R.M., Cheney, D.L. & Marler, P., "Monkey Responses to
  Three Different Alarm Calls: Evidence of Predator Classification and Semantic
  Communication", *Science* 210(4471):801–803, 1980.
  https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.7433999

## 6. Wolf pack hunting — and how little coordination it may require

### The case

- **What happens:** Wolf packs (*Canis lupus*) pursue elk, bison and moose in
  groups, appearing to take complementary positions — some animals holding the
  prey's head, others attacking flanks. On the standard telling this is
  role-differentiated teamwork.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** If roles are real, the animal taking the
  head position bears more risk than the flankers, and the question is what stops
  the flankers from hanging back. The complication is that Muro and colleagues
  reproduced the main features of pack hunting — tracking, pursuit, encirclement
  — in simulation from two decentralized rules per wolf: move toward the prey
  until a minimum safe distance, and once there move away from other wolves at
  that distance. No communication, no roles, no leader. That does not prove wolves
  work this way, but it shows that apparent teamwork is not evidence of teamwork.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape*, as a second methodological
  caution alongside the meerkats. Session 2, *Some basic game theory*, on
  emergent pattern versus intentional coordination.
- **Footage:** Excellent. Yellowstone wolf-elk and Wood Buffalo wolf-bison
  sequences in BBC *Frozen Planet* (2011) and *Planet Earth* (2006, "Ice
  Worlds"), and extensive Yellowstone Wolf Project archive. The *Frozen Planet*
  wolf-and-bison chase in "Summer" is the most-used single sequence.
- **Source:** Muro, C., Escobedo, R., Spector, L. & Coppinger, R.P., "Wolf-pack
  (*Canis lupus*) hunting strategies emerge from simple rules in computational
  simulations", *Behavioural Processes* 88(3):192–197, 2011.
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21963347/ For the field behavior, Mech, L.D. &
  Boitani, L. (eds), *Wolves: Behavior, Ecology, and Conservation* (2003).

## 7. Lions: leaders, laggards and the absence of punishment

### The case

- **What happens:** Heinsohn and Packer played recordings of intruding lions to
  Serengeti prides and watched which females advanced. Individuals were
  consistent across trials. Some always led, some participated only when least
  needed, some never participated at all. Leaders looked back at laggards —
  they evidently registered who was hanging back — and did nothing about it.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Territorial defense is a public good
  with real risk attached and a straightforwardly available free ride. This is
  the case where the standard fix fails: there is no punishment, so reciprocity
  is not what maintains the behavior, and the authors said plainly that existing
  theory does not explain it. Four stable strategy types coexisting in one group
  is a better picture of real cooperation than any account where everybody
  contributes.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape*, and *Repeated interaction
  and the folk theorem* — this is the negative case for the punishment
  requirement. Runs well through the diagnostic: repeated interaction yes,
  observable defection yes, punishment no.
- **Footage:** Good for lion territorial and hunting behavior generally — BBC
  *Dynasties* (2018) has a lion episode; *Planet Earth II* "Deserts" has the
  Namib lions; *Big Cat Diary* has years of Mara material. The specific playback
  experiments were filmed by the Serengeti Lion Project but the research footage
  is not packaged for teaching. Use general pride footage and narrate the
  experiment over it.
- **Source:** Heinsohn, R. & Packer, C., "Complex Cooperative Strategies in
  Group-Territorial African Lions", *Science* 269(5228):1260–1262, 1995.
  https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.7652573

## 8. Orca wave-washing

### The case

- **What happens:** A small population of Antarctic killer whales (*Orcinus
  orca*, pack ice ecotype B) hunt Weddell seals on ice floes by swimming in
  formation beneath the floe and surfacing together to generate a wave that
  washes the seal off. Attacks are repeated and adjusted between attempts —
  breaking a large floe into smaller pieces first, then washing off the
  remainder.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The wave cannot be produced alone; the
  minimum viable group is several animals swimming in synchrony, and the prey is
  then shared. It is a threshold public good in which the good is indivisible
  and the technique is culturally transmitted — Pitman's estimate is that fewer
  than about 100 animals worldwide do it, passed down within matrilines. The
  cultural-transmission angle sets up Session 5 directly.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape*. Session 5, *Multi-level
  selection*, as non-human cultural transmission of a cooperation technology.
- **Footage:** Excellent — among the best wildlife footage ever shot of a
  coordinated hunt. BBC *Frozen Planet* (2011), episode "Spring", filmed with
  Robert Pitman as scientific adviser. Cut as "Killer Whales Working Together to
  Hunt Seals on Ice | BBC Earth" on YouTube, roughly 4 minutes.
  https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g1VEwsI4SlY
- **Source:** Pitman, R.L. & Durban, J.W., "Cooperative hunting behavior, prey
  selectivity and prey handling by pack ice killer whales (*Orcinus orca*), type
  B, in Antarctic Peninsula waters", *Marine Mammal Science* 28(1):16–36, 2012.

## 9. Humpback bubble-net feeding

### The case

- **What happens:** Humpback whales (*Megaptera novaeangliae*) in Southeast
  Alaska and British Columbia form groups that encircle herring beneath the
  surface with a spiral curtain of bubbles, then lunge up through it together
  with mouths open. Crittercam work indicates division of labor: some animals
  blow the net, others dive to drive fish upward, others vocalize to herd them.
  Working groups are small and stable, with associations persisting over years
  and in some cases decades.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A public good — the net — produced
  jointly, benefiting all participants, and impossible alone. Stable membership
  over decades is the shadow of the future doing exactly what the folk theorem
  says it should. Fewer than 10% of the Southeast Alaska humpbacks do it, and
  recent work tracks the behavior spreading through the Canadian Pacific
  population, which makes it a second case of cultural diffusion.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape* and *Repeated interaction and
  the folk theorem* — stable partners, repeated play. Session 5,
  *Multi-level selection*.
- **Footage:** Excellent and abundant. Filmed in BBC *The Blue Planet* (2001)
  and in Alaskan whale-watch and research footage from the Alaska Whale
  Foundation; aerial drone footage of the full bubble spiral from above is now
  common and reads much better on a slide than surface-level footage.
- **Source:** Wiley, D. et al., "Underwater components of humpback whale
  bubble-net feeding behaviour", *Behaviour* 148(5–6):575–602, 2011. On diffusion:
  "The diffusion of cooperative and solo bubble net feeding in Canadian Pacific
  humpback whales", *Proc. R. Soc. B* 293(2063), 2026.

## 10. Chimpanzee cooperative hunting of red colobus

### The case

- **What happens:** Chimpanzee (*Pan troglodytes*) parties hunt red colobus
  monkeys. At Taï in Côte d'Ivoire, Boesch describes animals taking
  differentiated roles — driver, blockers, ambusher — in continuous canopy where
  the prey cannot be cornered without them. At Gombe, hunts look much more like
  parallel individual pursuit in broken canopy where a lone hunter does well.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Meat is the payoff and it is divisible,
  so the question is whether hunters get more than non-hunters. At Taï, Boesch
  reports selective meat sharing that gives hunters a positive net caloric
  return while bystanders get little — a selective incentive in Olson's sense,
  attached to participation. At Gombe no such mechanism operates and cooperation
  is correspondingly unstable. **Flag the dispute plainly:** whether Taï hunting
  is genuinely coordinated role-taking or individual hunting that looks
  coordinated is contested between Boesch on one side and Gilby, Watts and
  others on the other, and the difference between sites may be ecological rather
  than social. The disagreement is itself the teaching point — the same behavior
  supports cooperation or not depending on whether a sharing rule rewards
  participants.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape* and *Olson* — this is
  selective incentives in a non-human species. Session 5, *Multi-level
  selection*.
- **Footage:** Excellent. BBC *Trials of Life* (1990), episode "Hunting and
  Escaping", has the Taï-style ambush sequence narrated by Attenborough, cut on
  YouTube as "Chimps Set Up an Ambush for Monkeys | Trials Of Life | BBC Earth".
  Netflix *Chimp Empire* (2023, dir. James Reed), filmed over 18 months at Ngogo
  in Kibale, has modern high-definition colobus hunts. Boesch's own Taï research
  footage exists and is partly public through the Max Planck Institute.
- **Source:** Boesch, C., "Cooperative hunting roles among Taï chimpanzees",
  *Human Nature* 13:27–46, 2002. Boesch, C. & Boesch-Achermann, H., *The
  Chimpanzees of the Taï Forest* (2000). For the counter-position: Gilby, I.C. et
  al., "The meat-scrap hypothesis", *Behav. Ecol. Sociobiol.* 63:1821–1832, 2009;
  Gilby, I.C. et al., "'Impact hunters' catalyse cooperative hunting in two wild
  chimpanzee communities", *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B* 370:20150005, 2015.

## 11. Groupers and moray eels hunting together

### The case

- **What happens:** Roving coral groupers (*Plectropomus pessuliferus
  marisrubri*) in the Red Sea swim to a resting giant moray eel (*Gymnothorax
  javanicus*) and perform a rapid head-shake at close range. The eel leaves its
  crevice and the two hunt together. The eel can enter crevices; the grouper
  takes fish in open water; prey has nowhere to go. Groupers also perform a
  headstand-and-point signal directed at hidden prey, which recruits the
  partner to that spot.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** An interspecific mutualism with
  complementary rather than shared capabilities, so the joint output exceeds the
  sum of the parts. Neither animal shares the catch — each eats what it catches
  — which removes the division problem entirely and is why the partnership is
  stable without any enforcement. A useful counterexample: not every cooperation
  problem is a dilemma. Vail's analysis is also the first demonstration of
  referential gesture outside large-brained vertebrates.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape*. Session 2, *The games*, as a
  case with no defection temptation to speak of — good for showing what the
  payoff structure has to look like for cooperation to be easy.
- **Footage:** Excellent. BBC *Blue Planet II* (2017), episode 3 "Coral Reefs",
  carries the closely related coral trout and octopus partnership with the
  grouper's pointing display, filmed on the Great Barrier Reef. Research footage
  from the Bshary and Vail work in the Red Sea accompanies both papers as
  supplementary video. BBC Earth has short cuts of the reef sequence.
- **Source:** Bshary, R. et al., "Interspecific Communicative and Coordinated
  Hunting between Groupers and Giant Moray Eels in the Red Sea", *PLoS Biology*
  4(12):e431, 2006. Vail, A.L., Manica, A. & Bshary, R., "Referential gestures in
  fish collaborative hunting", *Nature Communications* 4:1765, 2013.
  https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms2781

## 12. Cleaner wrasse and their clients

### The case

- **What happens:** Bluestreak cleaner wrasse (*Labroides dimidiatus*) hold
  fixed cleaning stations on reefs. Client fish — including predators that could
  eat them — queue, adopt a posture inviting inspection, and hold still while the
  wrasse removes ectoparasites, including from inside the mouth and gills. A
  single wrasse may handle over 2,000 client visits a day.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Mutualism with a large and continuous
  temptation to defect on both sides — the client could eat the cleaner, the
  cleaner could take the more nutritious mucus. Location is what makes it work:
  the station is fixed, both parties know where to find each other, interactions
  repeat, and clients waiting in the queue can watch. Repeated interaction,
  observability and the option to switch or punish — the full folk theorem
  ingredient list, running on a reef.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem*. This
  is the best non-human illustration of the shadow of the future, and it maps
  onto the diagnostic question by question.
- **Footage:** Excellent. Cleaning stations are among the most-filmed reef
  behaviors. BBC *The Blue Planet* (2001) and *Blue Planet II* (2017) both carry
  sequences, as does BBC *Trials of Life* (1990, "Living Together"). Underwater
  cleaning-station footage is also abundant in public dive archives.
- **Source:** Grutter, A.S., "Parasite removal rates by the cleaner wrasse
  *Labroides dimidiatus*", *Marine Ecology Progress Series* 130:61–70, 1996.
  Bshary, R. & Grutter, A.S., "Asymmetric cheating opportunities and partner
  control in a cleaner fish mutualism", *Animal Behaviour* 63:547–555, 2002.

## 13. Cleaner wrasse cheating, and what clients do about it

### The case

- **What happens:** Cleaners prefer client mucus to parasites. When they take
  mucus, the client gives a visible jolt — an abrupt body twitch — which is the
  observable signature of cheating and is how researchers score service quality.
  Clients respond according to their options: visiting clients with access to
  several stations switch to a different cleaner next time, while resident
  clients with only one local station chase and punish the cleaner instead.
  Cleaners also give better service when other clients are watching, and
  cooperating male-female cleaner pairs include males punishing females who
  cheat — which turns out to serve the male's own interest in the client staying.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The cleaner is a free rider on its own
  reputation. The mechanisms that hold it in check are exactly the ones the
  course names for humans: exit, punishment, and reputation among observers. The
  behavioral response is contingent on which of those the client actually has
  available, which is the diagnostic being run by a fish.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem*, and
  *The cooperative ape*. Session 4, *Did it ever work?* — the exit-versus-voice
  distinction is directly transferable.
- **Footage:** Good but needs care. The cleaning interaction itself is
  extensively filmed (entry 12). The *jolt* is visible in close-up cleaning
  footage once you know to look for it, which makes it a strong live-annotation
  moment: play a cleaning sequence, point at the twitch. Bshary's laboratory and
  field footage from Ras Mohammed and Lizard Island accompanies the papers.
- **Source:** Bshary, R. & Grutter, A.S., "Punishment and partner switching cause
  cooperative behaviour in a cleaning mutualism", *Biology Letters* 1:396–399,
  2005. Bshary, R. & Grutter, A.S., "Image scoring and cooperation in a cleaner
  fish mutualism", *Nature* 441:975–978, 2006. Raihani, N.J., Grutter, A.S. &
  Bshary, R., "Punishers benefit from third-party punishment in fish", *Science*
  327(5962):171, 2010.

## 14. Vampire bat blood sharing

### The case

- **What happens:** Common vampire bats (*Desmodus rotundus*) starve within
  about 60 hours of a failed feed. Bats that fed regurgitate blood to roostmates
  that did not. Wilkinson's 1984 field and captive work found donations
  predicted by an index of opportunity for reciprocation as well as by
  relatedness, and later work by Carter and Wilkinson found that previous help
  received predicted donations better than either relatedness or harassment.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The textbook case of direct reciprocity
  in a non-human animal, and one where the asymmetric payoff makes the trade
  worthwhile — the donor gives up a few hours of buffer, the recipient avoids
  death. Bats roost together for years, recognize individuals, and can withhold
  from non-reciprocators. Repetition, recognition, sanction.
- **Flag the dispute:** the reciprocity reading has been contested. In the wild
  data, 95% of observed sharing was between close kin, and 70% of 110
  observations were mothers feeding pups. Several authors argued the behavior is
  maintained by indirect fitness alone, with non-kin sharing explained as kin
  recognition error, indiscriminate altruism, or harassment. Carter and
  Wilkinson's controlled work is the reply, and it is a reply worth stating
  rather than assuming. Use the case, but do not present it as settled.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape* and *Repeated interaction and
  the folk theorem*. This is where reciprocity gets introduced.
- **Footage:** Moderate, and worse than the case deserves. Regurgitation is
  filmed in captive colonies — Gerald Wilkinson's and Gerald Carter's lab
  footage, and colony footage from the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute in
  Panama. BBC *Life of Mammals* (2002) and various Nat Geo bat documentaries
  carry vampire bat feeding sequences, but sharing footage is shorter and less
  cinematic than the hunting footage. Expect 30 seconds of usable film, not
  three minutes.
- **Source:** Wilkinson, G.S., "Reciprocal food sharing in the vampire bat",
  *Nature* 308:181–184, 1984. https://www.nature.com/articles/308181a0 Carter,
  G.G. & Wilkinson, G.S., "Food sharing in vampire bats: reciprocal help predicts
  donations more than relatedness or harassment", *Proc. R. Soc. B*
  280:20122573, 2013. Carter, G.G. & Wilkinson, G.S., "Does food sharing in
  vampire bats demonstrate reciprocity?", *Communicative & Integrative Biology*
  6:e25783, 2013.

## 15. Honeybee worker policing

### The case

- **What happens:** Honeybee (*Apis mellifera*) workers cannot mate but retain
  functional ovaries and can lay unfertilized male eggs. They mostly do not, and
  when they do, other workers eat the eggs. Ratnieks and Visscher showed strong
  discrimination against worker-laid male eggs in queenright colonies — over 99%
  removed within a day, against a much lower rate for queen-laid eggs.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The colony is not a harmonious
  superorganism; it is a group with a real internal conflict of interest that is
  suppressed by enforcement. A worker is more related to her own son than to the
  queen's sons, so selfish reproduction pays her — and because the queen mates
  with 10–20 males, each worker is more related to the queen's sons than to
  another worker's sons, so it pays everyone else to stop her. Policing is
  decentralized, mutual, and continuous. Mutual coercion, mutually agreed upon,
  with no agreement.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape* — the entry that punctures the
  assumption that eusocial insects have solved the problem. Session 4,
  *Coercion*, as the non-human precedent for Hardin's actual prescription.
- **Footage:** Moderate. Honeybee comb behavior is extremely well filmed —
  BBC *Life in the Undergrowth* (2005), *Life* (2009), and the observation-hive
  tradition going back to von Frisch. Egg-eating specifically is a close-up
  microscopy shot rather than a documentary sequence; research footage exists in
  the Ratnieks lab (Laboratory of Apiculture and Social Insects, Sussex). The
  waggle dance footage is the reliable crowd-pleaser if you need bees on screen.
- **Source:** Ratnieks, F.L.W. & Visscher, P.K., "Worker policing in the
  honeybee", *Nature* 342:796–797, 1989.
  https://www.nature.com/articles/342796a0 Wenseleers, T. & Ratnieks, F.L.W.,
  "Comparative analysis of worker reproduction and policing in eusocial
  Hymenoptera supports relatedness theory", *American Naturalist* 168:E163–E179,

## 16. Army ant living bridges

### The case

- **What happens:** *Eciton burchellii* army ants on the march link their own
  bodies into bridges spanning gaps in the trail. The bridges assemble in
  seconds, then move — lengthening, widening and sliding across the gap to
  shorten the route. They stop moving at the point where the number of workers
  locked into the structure costs more than the shortcut saves, and they
  disassemble when traffic over them falls below a threshold.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The bridge is a public good made of the
  contributors themselves, and every ant in it is an ant not foraging. The
  colony solves a cost-benefit optimization with no ant able to perceive either
  side of it — the trade-off is implemented entirely by local rules about traffic
  and contact. A useful case for showing that optimal collective outcomes do not
  require anyone to understand the problem.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape*. Session 4,
  *Polycentricity*, as an argument that coordination need not be centralized.
- **Footage:** Good. BBC *Planet Earth* (2006, "Jungles") and *Life in the
  Undergrowth* (2005) both carry *Eciton* raid columns and bridging. Reid's
  Princeton/NJIT fieldwork on Barro Colorado Island produced high-speed video
  published as supplementary material with the paper and widely re-cut by
  Princeton and National Geographic. 1–2 minutes of very strong film.
- **Source:** Reid, C.R. et al., "Army ants dynamically adjust living bridges in
  response to a cost–benefit trade-off", *PNAS* 112(49):15113–15118, 2015.
  https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1512241112

## 17. Naked mole-rats

### The case

- **What happens:** Naked mole-rats (*Heterocephalus glaber*) live in
  subterranean colonies of up to 300 in which a single female breeds with one to
  three males and every other animal is reproductively suppressed, doing
  digging, foraging and defense instead. Jarvis identified this in 1981 as
  eusociality in a mammal — overlapping generations, cooperative brood care, a
  reproductive division of labor.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Complete reproductive self-abnegation by
  most of the group, which is as extreme as cooperation gets. It is held in place
  by a combination of high relatedness and active suppression — the queen shoves
  subordinates, and recent work identifies a queen-borne chemical signal
  (isopropyl myristate) mediating the suppression. Again: not harmony,
  enforcement. Pair with honeybee policing to make the point twice in different
  clades.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape*. Session 5, *Multi-level
  selection*, on relatedness and group-level selection.
- **Footage:** Good. Naked mole-rats are a zoo-exhibit staple and tunnel colonies
  are filmed in cutaway acrylic burrows — footage exists from the Smithsonian
  National Zoo, Pacific Science Center and the Zoological Society of London.
  BBC *Life of Mammals* (2002) and *Life* (2009) both include them. Visually
  striking in a way that holds a room.
- **Source:** Jarvis, J.U.M., "Eusociality in a Mammal: Cooperative Breeding in
  Naked Mole-Rat Colonies", *Science* 212(4494):571–573, 1981.
  https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.7209555 On suppression: "A queen
  odour mediates reproductive suppression in a eusocial mammal", 2025.

## 18. Dictyostelium: single cells building a body

### The case

- **What happens:** The social amoeba *Dictyostelium discoideum* lives as
  independent single cells eating bacteria in soil. On starvation, tens of
  thousands aggregate into a motile slug and then a fruiting body: roughly 20% of
  the cells die to form a rigid stalk, lifting the remaining 80% into spores that
  can disperse. The stalk cells get nothing. They are dead.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The cleanest case of a collective action
  problem with no brain, no communication in any meaningful sense, and an
  absolutely terminal cost to contributors. It also puts the multicellularity
  question in the course's own terms: a body is a group of cells that solved a
  collective action problem, and the solution is mostly clonality — cells in a
  fruiting body are usually near-identical, so the stalk cell's genes go into the
  spores anyway.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape*, opening it — cooperation
  pre-dates animals by a long way. Session 5, *Multi-level selection*, since this
  is a major evolutionary transition in miniature.
- **Footage:** Excellent, and cheap to obtain. Aggregation and fruiting-body
  time-lapse is a classic microscopy sequence dating to the Bonner films of the
  1940s–50s; Princeton hosts John Bonner's original *Dictyostelium* movies
  publicly. Modern time-lapse is available from the Queller–Strassmann lab at
  Washington University and from MBL teaching collections. Roughly 60–90 seconds
  of time-lapse, and it is genuinely arresting on a screen.
- **Source:** Bonner, J.T., *The Cellular Slime Molds* (1967). Strassmann, J.E.,
  Zhu, Y. & Queller, D.C., "Altruism and social cheating in the social amoeba
  *Dictyostelium discoideum*", *Nature* 408:965–967, 2000.

## 19. Dictyostelium cheater strains

### The case

- **What happens:** When two genetically distinct *Dictyostelium* strains are
  mixed, they still form a single chimeric fruiting body — and in about half of
  such chimeras one strain contributes disproportionately few cells to the stalk
  and disproportionately many to the spores. Some lab-derived mutants are
  obligate cheaters that cannot form a stalk at all and can only reproduce by
  exploiting a partner.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** This is the entry that does the most
  work in the whole file. A free rider in a system with no cognition, no
  intention, no culture and no morality. Whatever defection is, it is not a human
  character defect — it is what happens whenever the payoff to withholding
  exceeds the payoff to contributing, in any substrate. Obligate cheaters are
  also limited by their own success: they produce shortened stalks, which caps
  how far they can spread, which is a natural-experiment version of the point
  that defection is self-limiting at high frequency.
- **Could serve:** Session 2, *Some basic game theory* — the argument that
  defection is rational rather than wicked, made with something that cannot be
  wicked. Session 3, *The cooperative ape*.
- **Footage:** Moderate. The same time-lapse as entry 18 covers the mechanism;
  the cheating itself is shown through fluorescently labeled strains in chimeric
  fruiting bodies — still images and short microscopy clips rather than film.
  Available in figures and supplementary video from the Queller–Strassmann lab.
  This one may work better as a diagram than as footage.
- **Source:** Strassmann, J.E., Zhu, Y. & Queller, D.C., *Nature* 408:965–967,
  2000. Khare, A. et al., "Cheating by exploitation of developmental prestalk
  patterning in *Dictyostelium discoideum*", *PLoS Genetics* 6:e1000854, 2010.
  Also "In the social amoeba *Dictyostelium discoideum*, shortened stalks may
  limit obligate cheater success", *PeerJ* 12:e17118, 2024.

## 20. Pseudomonas siderophores: a public good and its cheats

### The case

- **What happens:** *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* cells secrete pyoverdine, a
  molecule that scavenges scarce iron from the environment. Making it is costly.
  The iron-pyoverdine complex is then available to any cell with the right
  receptor, including cells that make none. Griffin, West and Buckling showed
  that non-producing cheats invade producer populations, and that they do best in
  well-mixed conditions where the good really is shared, while spatial structure
  favors producers.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A textbook public good — non-excludable,
  costly to produce, freely consumed — in a system of bacteria. The experimental
  result is the one the course needs: cooperation survives when interaction is
  local and neighbors are relatives, and collapses when the population is well
  mixed. That is Nowak's spatial structure result, run in a flask, and it is the
  most direct available evidence for the claim that structure rather than
  virtue determines outcomes.
- **Could serve:** Session 2, *The games*, on public goods and free riding.
  Session 3, *The evolution of cooperation*, as the wet-lab version of the
  spatial-grid result.
- **Footage:** Poor. This is a plate-and-flask experiment; the visible output is
  fluorescent colonies and a growth curve. There is public footage of
  siderophore-producing colonies under UV (pyoverdine fluoresces) from
  microbiology teaching channels, but nothing that carries a room. **Use a
  diagram, not film.** Included because the science is load-bearing, not because
  it can be shown.
- **Source:** Griffin, A.S., West, S.A. & Buckling, A., "Cooperation and
  competition in pathogenic bacteria", *Nature* 430:1024–1027, 2004. West, S.A.
  et al., "The social lives of microbes", *Annual Review of Ecology, Evolution,
  and Systematics* 38:53–77, 2007.

## 21. Myxococcus xanthus developmental cheats

### The case

- **What happens:** *Myxococcus xanthus* is a soil bacterium that hunts in
  swarms and, on starvation, aggregates into multicellular fruiting bodies in
  which some cells become spores. Velicer, Kroos and Lenski isolated mutants
  that are defective at fruiting-body development in isolation but,
  when mixed with wild type, over-represent themselves in the spore pool at the
  wild type's expense.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A second, independent instance of the
  same structure as entry 19, in a bacterium rather than an amoeba. Two
  unrelated lineages independently evolving both multicellular cooperation and
  the cheating that exploits it is a stronger claim than either alone: the
  problem is in the payoff structure, not in the organism.
- **Could serve:** Session 2, *Some basic game theory*. Session 3, *The
  cooperative ape*. Use alongside 19 rather than instead of it; the point of
  having both is convergence.
- **Footage:** Moderate. *Myxococcus* swarming and rippling time-lapse is
  genuinely good film — visible waves moving across an agar plate — and is
  available from the Velicer lab (ETH Zurich) and various microbiology labs.
  Fruiting-body formation time-lapse also exists. The cheating itself is not
  filmable, only the assay.
- **Source:** Velicer, G.J., Kroos, L. & Lenski, R.E., "Developmental cheating in
  the social bacterium *Myxococcus xanthus*", *Nature* 404:598–601, 2000.
  https://www.nature.com/articles/35007066

## 22. Physarum network optimization

### The case

- **What happens:** The plasmodial slime mould *Physarum polycephalum* is a
  single enormous multinucleate cell that spreads as a network of tubes. Placed
  on a map of the Tokyo region with oat flakes at the positions of the
  surrounding cities, it grew a network comparable to the actual Tokyo rail
  system in efficiency, cost and fault tolerance. It also solves mazes by
  retracting tubes on dead-end routes.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A boundary case, and worth including for
  exactly that reason. There is no group here — it is one cell — so there is no
  free-rider problem at all, and that is the point: the infrastructure problem
  gets solved effortlessly precisely because the units cannot have divergent
  interests. Set next to entry 19, where the same organism-type in cellular form
  *does* have divergent interests and *does* get cheated, it isolates the
  variable. Also a clean illustration of distributed optimization with no
  planner, which Session 4's polycentricity module needs.
- **Could serve:** Session 4, *Polycentricity*. Session 3, *The cooperative ape*,
  as the contrast case.
- **Footage:** Excellent. *Physarum* time-lapse is widely filmed — the Tokyo rail
  experiment itself was filmed and published as supplementary video with the
  *Science* paper and re-cut by numerous outlets; the Paris Zoological Park's
  "blob" exhibit (2019) generated substantial broadcast footage; BBC *Planet
  Earth II* and *Green Planet* carry slime mould time-lapse. 60 seconds of
  time-lapse of a network forming is unusually compelling television.
- **Source:** Tero, A. et al., "Rules for Biologically Inspired Adaptive Network
  Design", *Science* 327(5964):439–442, 2010. Nakagaki, T., Yamada, H. & Tóth,
  Á., "Maze-solving by an amoeboid organism", *Nature* 407:470, 2000.

## 23. Legume–rhizobium host sanctions

### The case

- **What happens:** Legumes house rhizobial bacteria in root nodules and feed
  them carbon; the bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen for the plant. Kiers and
  colleagues forced a normally cooperative strain to stop fixing nitrogen by
  replacing the air around one half of a split root system with an argon-oxygen
  mixture containing no N₂. The plant cut the oxygen supply to those nodules and
  the reproductive success of the bacteria inside fell by about half.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A mutualism between organisms with no
  shared interest whatever, stabilized by the host detecting under-performance
  and sanctioning it at the level of the individual nodule. Detection and
  punishment, applied locally, with no central authority and no capacity on
  either side to negotiate. This is the closest thing in biology to a
  well-designed institution, and it is the answer to "why doesn't every mutualism
  collapse into cheating".
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *Ostrom and the commons* — monitoring and graduated
  sanctions, two of the design principles, running in a root. Session 4,
  *Coercion*.
- **Footage:** Poor to moderate. Nodules can be photographed and dissected — pink
  leghemoglobin interiors are visually distinctive — and there is agricultural
  extension footage of nodule inspection. The experiment itself is a gas manifold
  and a spreadsheet. **Use a diagram.** Strong case, weak film.
- **Source:** Kiers, E.T., Rousseau, R.A., West, S.A. & Denison, R.F., "Host
  sanctions and the legume–rhizobium mutualism", *Nature* 425:78–81, 2003.
  https://www.nature.com/articles/nature01931

## 24. Acacia ants and the parasite of their mutualism

### The case

- **What happens:** Swollen-thorn acacias in Central America house *Pseudomyrmex*
  ants, providing hollow thorns to live in and nectar and protein bodies to eat;
  the ants attack herbivores and prune encroaching vegetation. *Pseudomyrmex
  nigropilosa* occupies the same acacias, eats the same rewards, and does not
  defend the tree. Janzen documented it in 1975 as a parasite of a mutualism.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A free rider in a two-species mutualism,
  and — more interestingly — a case where the host has counter-adaptations. The
  acacia's nectar is sucrose-free, which is unattractive to generalist ants;
  mutualist workers lose the sucrose-cleaving enzyme invertase during
  development, so they are locked into their host, while exploiters retain a
  preference for sucrose. High-reward host trees are exploited less and defended
  better. The host is, in effect, screening for partners with no exit option.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape*. Session 4, *Changing the
  payoffs* — the acacia's answer to cheating is to change what the reward is,
  which is the subsidy-design argument in miniature.
- **Footage:** Good. Acacia ants swarming out at a disturbance is a standard and
  very effective sequence — BBC *Private Life of Plants* (1995) and *Life* (2009,
  "Plants") both carry ant-acacia material, as does *Planet Earth II*
  ("Jungles"). The distinction between defenders and the non-defending parasite
  is not filmed as such and would need narration over generic footage.
- **Source:** Janzen, D.H., "*Pseudomyrmex nigropilosa*: A Parasite of a
  Mutualism", *Science* 188(4191):936–937, 1975. Heil, M. et al., "Divergent
  investment strategies of *Acacia* myrmecophytes and the coexistence of
  mutualists and exploiters", *PNAS* 106:18091–18096, 2009. Kautz, S. et al.,
  "How to prevent cheating: a digestive specialization ties mutualistic
  plant-ants to their ant-plant partners", *Evolution* 63:839–853, 2009.

## 25. Honeyguides and human honey hunters

### The case

- **What happens:** Greater honeyguides (*Indicator indicator*) lead human
  honey-hunters to wild bees' nests; the humans open the nest, take the honey,
  and the bird eats the wax and larvae it cannot reach alone. Spottiswoode showed
  the communication runs both ways: the Yao honey-hunters' specific "brrr-hm"
  call raised the probability of being guided from about 33% to 66% and the
  probability of finding a nest from 17% to 54%, against control sounds of
  similar volume. Hadza honey-hunters in Tanzania use a whistle instead, and the
  birds respond to their local human signal.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A mutualism between a wild animal and
  humans that is unique among living species, and which runs on a learned signal
  on both sides. The division is clean — humans cannot climb to what the bird
  finds, the bird cannot open what the humans open — so the temptation to defect
  is small, though honey-hunters in some cultures traditionally burn or hide the
  wax to keep the bird hungry, which is a documented instance of the human side
  cheating.
- **Could serve:** Session 3, *The cooperative ape*, as the bridge from animal to
  human cooperation — literally the same case on both sides. Session 5, *Henrich
  and the trust of strangers*, on culturally transmitted signals.
- **Footage:** Good. Spottiswoode's fieldwork in the Niassa Special Reserve was
  filmed and released with the 2016 *Science* paper as supplementary video, and
  widely re-cut by the BBC, National Geographic and NPR. The BBC *Natural
  Histories* radio programme covers it with Spottiswoode in audio. Expect 2–3
  minutes of usable film showing the call, the bird leading, and the nest opened.
- **Source:** Spottiswoode, C.N., Begg, K.S. & Begg, C.M., "Reciprocal signaling
  in honeyguide-human mutualism", *Science* 353(6297):387–389, 2016.
  https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aaf4885 Wood, B.M. et al.,
  "Mutualism and manipulation in Hadza-honeyguide interactions", *Evolution and
  Human Behavior* 35:540–546, 2014.

## Notes for selection

**The three with the best footage,** if only three make the cut: the drongo
false alarm (entry 4), orca wave-washing (entry 8), and Battle at Kruger (entry
2). All three are 4–8 minutes, all three are available now, and between them
they cover cooperation working, cooperation being cheated, and cooperation as
something nobody planned.

**The three that do the most argumentative work,** which is not the same list:
*Dictyostelium* cheaters (19), the selfish sentinel correction (3), and the
lion laggards (7). These are the entries that establish defection as structural
and prevent the module from becoming a parade of charming animals.

**Cases deliberately not included.** Ant "agriculture" and termite mounds — well
filmed, but the collective action structure is nearly trivial given clonality,
so they illustrate less than they appear to. Dolphin cooperative feeding — the
footage is good, but the coordination claims in the popular literature run well
ahead of what is established. Emperor penguin huddling — excellent footage, and
there is a real free-rider question about position in the huddle, but the
literature on whether outer positions are exploited or rotated is not settled
enough to state cleanly.
