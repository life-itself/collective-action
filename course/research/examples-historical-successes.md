# Historical successes — candidate examples

Candidates only. Nothing here is written up for teaching yet, and several entries
carry a live scholarly dispute that would need resolving before use.

Scope: pre-1990. Post-1990 AI, climate and internet governance cases are a
separate list.

A note on skepticism, since it applies to a third of what follows. The
long-enduring commons literature has an idealisation problem: cases get
described by the rules on the books rather than by what people did, exclusion of
outsiders gets quietly dropped from the account, and institutions that survived
partly through coercion get remembered as consensual. Where an account is
contested I say so in the entry rather than at the end.

## Commons governed over long periods

### Törbel, Valais, Switzerland

- **What happened:** An alpine village in the Swiss Valais has held its summer
  grazing meadows, forests, waste land, irrigation channels and paths as
  communal property under written rules. A charter of 1224 is the earliest
  document of the rules; irrigation arrangements are documented from 1483. The
  binding rule on grazing is the *wintering rule*: no member may send more cows
  to the alp than he can feed through the winter on his own land.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Subtractable commons, with the
  appropriation limit set by a proxy that is cheap to verify. The mechanism is
  **observability engineered into the rule itself**. Counting how much grass a
  cow eats on a shared alp is impossible; counting a neighbour's hay barn in
  February is trivial, and everyone in a village of 600 does it as a side effect
  of living there. Reinforced by repetition (the same households, indefinitely),
  and by the fact that the commoners' association is a closed membership
  corporation — new residents don't automatically get grazing rights.
- **Could serve:** *Ostrom and the commons* (S3); *Introduction to collective
  action* (S1), as an opening success paired with a failure.
- **Media:** Netting's book carries photographs and the village record series.
  Swissinfo produced a short video piece on Törbel and Ostrom (2025). I haven't
  verified whether a full documentary exists.
- **Source:** Robert McC. Netting, *Balancing on an Alp: Ecological Change and
  Continuity in a Swiss Mountain Community* (Cambridge UP, 1981), from fieldwork
  in 1970–71; Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990), ch. 3.

### The huerta of Valencia and the Tribunal de las Aguas

- **What happened:** The irrigated plain around Valencia is served by canals
  (*acequias*) whose users elect officials and whose disputes are heard by a
  court of eight elected farmers, sitting in the open air outside the cathedral
  on Thursdays at noon. Proceedings are oral, in Valencian, and decisions carry
  no appeal. The court still sits.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Subtractable commons with the classic
  upstream/downstream asymmetry. The mechanism is **low-cost graduated
  sanctioning plus public shaming**: the canal guards are paid by the irrigators
  themselves and have an interest in catching theft, penalties start small, and
  the hearing is held in a public square where the offender's neighbours watch.
  Ostrom's emphasis is that the dispute resolution is fast and cheap enough that
  people actually use it, which is what keeps the rules alive.
- **Contested:** The popular claim that the tribunal is a thousand years old and
  runs continuously from al-Andalus is not documented. No text mentions the
  tribunal before the 18th century; the pre-modern continuity is inference, and
  the "oldest democratic institution in Europe" line is tourist-board framing.
  The irrigation communities themselves are much better evidenced than the court.
- **Could serve:** *Ostrom and the commons* (S3); *Polycentricity* (S4), since
  each canal governs itself and the tribunal sits above them.
- **Media:** The court sits in public and is filmed regularly; it was inscribed
  on UNESCO's intangible heritage list in 2009, and that listing has associated
  video. Good photographic material.
- **Source:** Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990), ch. 3; Thomas F. Glick,
  *Irrigation and Society in Medieval Valencia* (Harvard UP, 1970).

### Iriai forest commons, Japan

- **What happened:** Japanese villages held forest and grassland in common under
  *iriai* rights, with detailed rules on what could be taken, when, how, and in
  what quantity per household. Ostrom drew on the villages of Hirano, Nagaike
  and Yamanoka, documented by Margaret McKean. Opening days for a given product
  were announced and everyone harvested at once.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Subtractable commons. The mechanism is
  **synchronised harvesting as a monitoring device**. If everyone cuts thatch on
  the same declared day, everyone can see everyone else's pile, and stealth
  harvesting out of season is conspicuous by definition. Villages also paid
  detectives — two-person patrols, deliberately paired so neither could be
  bribed alone — and fines were graduated and recorded.
- **Contested:** Iriai has declined sharply since the mid-20th century as fuel
  switched away from firewood and rural populations fell. Presenting it as
  continuous to the present would be wrong; the interesting claim is about the
  Tokugawa and Meiji period, not about now.
- **Could serve:** *Ostrom and the commons* (S3); *Repeated interaction and the
  folk theorem* (S3).
- **Media:** None known to me in film. Historical photographs of village forest
  work presumably exist but I haven't verified a collection.
- **Source:** Margaret A. McKean, "Management of Traditional Common Lands
  (Iriaichi) in Japan", in *Making the Commons Work* (1992), and her earlier 1986
  work; Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990), ch. 3.

### Balinese subak and the water temples

- **What happened:** Rice irrigation in Bali is organised by *subak* — farmer
  associations tied to water temples — which coordinate planting schedules across
  a whole watershed. Stephen Lansing and James Kremer built a simulation showing
  that the temple-coordinated schedules sit near optimal on a trade-off between
  water sharing and pest control. During the Green Revolution push of the 1970s,
  agricultural planners overrode the temple schedules; pests and water shortfalls
  followed, and the schedules were restored.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A coordination problem rather than a
  dilemma, which makes it unusually useful for the course. The mechanism is
  **ritual as a synchronisation device with a real ecological function**.
  Synchronised planting starves pests of a continuous host, but staggering is
  better for water supply; the temple calendar is the compromise, and it holds
  because the schedule arrives as religious obligation rather than as a
  negotiated agreement that anyone could reopen each season.
- **Contested:** Stefan Helmreich (1999) criticised the simulation work as
  importing a functionalist and depoliticised picture of Balinese society;
  Lansing has replied. Others argue Lansing overstates the temples' exclusive
  role in irrigation management relative to other actors. Worth teaching with
  the dispute attached rather than as settled fact.
- **Could serve:** *Ostrom and the commons* (S3); *Ritual and the manufactured
  tribe* (S5); *Costly signals and Big Gods* (S5).
- **Media:** *The Goddess and the Computer* (1988), a documentary made with
  Lansing, directed by André Singer. Strong footage of the temples and terraces.
- **Source:** J. Stephen Lansing, *Priests and Programmers* (Princeton UP, 1991);
  Lansing and Kremer, "Emergent Properties of Balinese Water Temple Networks",
  *American Anthropologist* 95 (1993); critique: Stefan Helmreich, "Digitizing
  'Development'", *Critique of Anthropology* 19 (1999).

### The zanjeras of Ilocos Norte, Philippines

- **What happened:** Farmer-built and farmer-run irrigation societies in northern
  Luzon, some dating to the Spanish colonial period. A member acquires land
  rights by contributing labour to building the system, and holds shares
  (*atar*) that entitle him to water and oblige him to maintenance days. Plots
  are laid out so that each member holds strips in the head, middle and tail
  sections of the system.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The mechanism is **making the free rider
  problem structurally impossible by aligning holdings**. The usual failure in
  gravity irrigation is that head-enders have no reason to maintain the tail of
  the canal. Give every member land at both ends and the incentive to maintain
  the whole system is individual, not collective. Labour obligations are
  recorded, absences fined, and repeated absence costs the share itself.
- **Could serve:** *Ostrom and the commons* (S3); *Changing the payoffs* (S4) —
  it is a property-rights design, just not a private one.
- **Media:** None known.
- **Source:** Robert Y. Siy Jr., *Community Resource Management: Lessons from the
  Zanjera* (University of the Philippines Press, 1982); Ostrom, *Governing the
  Commons* (1990), ch. 3.

### The Alanya inshore fishery, Turkey

- **What happened:** About 100 fishers work a small inshore fishery on the
  Turkish Mediterranean coast. Through the 1970s, competition over the best
  sites caused hostility and occasional violence. From the early 1980s the local
  cooperative operated a system in which named fishing sites are listed, fishers
  draw lots at the start of the season for an initial site, and then rotate one
  site east each day until the end of January, then back west, so that everyone
  passes through every site as the fish migrate.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Subtractable commons resolved by
  **rotation, which converts a contest into a queue**. Nobody has to be
  monitored by an authority: the fisher due at a site tomorrow has every reason
  to notice someone occupying it today, so monitoring is a by-product of the
  allocation rule. Note the rules were devised locally and are not enforced by
  the state, though the cooperative gave them a venue.
- **Could serve:** *Ostrom and the commons* (S3); *Did it ever work?* (S4), next
  to a collapsed industrial fishery at several orders of magnitude larger.
- **Media:** None known.
- **Source:** Fikret Berkes, "Local-level management and the commons problem",
  *Marine Policy* 10 (1986); Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990), ch. 1.

### Maine lobster gangs

- **What happened:** Maine's lobster fishery is divided informally into harbour
  territories held by "gangs" of fishers. Entry requires sponsorship and years
  of tolerated presence; intrusion is answered first by warnings, then by
  half-hitching or cutting trap lines. Alongside this, Maine fishers pushed for
  and comply with conservation rules — minimum and maximum size, and notching
  the tails of egg-bearing females so they can never be landed.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The mechanism is **graduated informal
  punishment that never has to reach the courts**, layered under formal state
  rules the industry itself lobbied for. It also shows the cost: the same
  exclusion that sustains the stock is enforced by property destruction and
  occasionally violence, and access is effectively hereditary.
- **Contested:** How much of Maine's stock performance is due to gang
  territoriality versus the size and v-notch rules, water temperature, and the
  collapse of cod as a lobster predator, is genuinely unsettled.
- **Could serve:** *Ostrom and the commons* (S3); *Did it ever work?* (S4); the
  exclusion cost feeds *The bill* (S5).
- **Media:** Heavily photographed and filmed. Discovery's *Lobster Wars* (2007)
  is about offshore Georges Bank rather than the harbour gangs, so check before
  using. Acheson's books carry field material.
- **Source:** James M. Acheson, *The Lobster Gangs of Maine* (University Press of
  New England, 1988), and *Capturing the Commons* (2003).

### Dutch water boards (waterschappen)

- **What happened:** Regional bodies governing dikes, drainage and water levels
  in the Netherlands. The Hoogheemraadschap van Rijnland was established in 1255
  by Count William II of Holland, formalising roughly two centuries of informal
  local arrangements. Boards levied their own taxes, maintained dikes, and
  assigned each landholder a specific stretch to keep up. There were around 2,600
  boards by 1950; they have since been amalgamated, and they still exist with
  their own elections.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A public good where defection is fatal
  to everyone — one unmaintained dike section floods the whole polder. The
  mechanism is **assignment plus inspection plus the power to tax**: each
  stretch has a named person responsible, inspectors ride the dikes, and the
  board can raise money and coerce. This is the case where a commons institution
  visibly acquires state-like powers, which makes it the bridge between Session 3
  and *Coercion*.
- **Could serve:** *Coercion* (S4); *Polycentricity* (S4); *Ostrom and the
  commons* (S3).
- **Media:** Extensive archival material; the boards' own archives are public.
  No specific documentary verified.
- **Source:** William H. TeBrake, *Medieval Frontier: Culture and Ecology in
  Rijnland* (Texas A&M UP, 1985); Dutch Water Authorities, *The Dutch Water
  Authority Model* (2021) for the institutional description.

### The English open-field system

- **What happened:** Much of medieval and early modern England farmed in
  scattered strips across large open fields, with common grazing on the stubble
  after harvest, stinting rules on how many animals each household could turn
  out, and manorial courts that set and enforced the bylaws. The system operated
  for centuries before enclosure.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** This is the case Hardin's "tragedy of
  the commons" is usually taken to be about, and the historical record says the
  commons were stinted, not open access. The mechanism is **a court of the
  commoners themselves** — the manorial court roll records presentments by
  neighbours and small fines, year after year.
- **Contested:** Heavily. Whether open fields were efficient, how well stinting
  actually held, and whether enclosure raised productivity are all live disputes
  — Robert Allen, Deirdre McCloskey and others disagree substantially. Do not
  present this as a clean success; present it as the correction to Hardin's
  framing.
- **Could serve:** *The games* (S2), against the tragedy of the commons as
  usually taught; *Ostrom and the commons* (S3).
- **Media:** Ridge-and-furrow earthworks are visible and photographable across
  the Midlands; Laxton in Nottinghamshire still farms open fields and has a
  visitor centre.
- **Source:** Garrett Hardin, "The Tragedy of the Commons", *Science* 162 (1968),
  for the framing; Robert C. Allen, *Enclosure and the Yeoman* (Oxford UP, 1992);
  Susan Jane Buck Cox, "No Tragedy on the Commons", *Environmental Ethics* 7
  (1985).

### Los Angeles groundwater basins

- **What happened:** From the 1940s, pumpers drawing on groundwater basins under
  the Los Angeles area faced falling water tables and saltwater intrusion from
  the sea. Over roughly two decades they negotiated basin-by-basin settlements
  limiting extraction, created water districts and replenishment districts,
  litigated where necessary, and hired a neutral watermaster to measure and
  report pumping.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** This is Ostrom's own dissertation work
  and the empirical root of polycentricity. The mechanism is **nesting**:
  hundreds of independent pumpers were too many to bargain directly, so they
  built a stack of overlapping bodies — voluntary associations, special
  districts, courts, and a state agency — each handling a different part of the
  problem, with none of them in charge of the whole. Also a clean case of
  **third-party measurement making agreement possible**: nobody trusted anyone's
  self-reported pumping until an independent watermaster measured it.
- **Could serve:** *Polycentricity* (S4) — this is the best-documented case for
  it; *Did it ever work?* (S4).
- **Media:** None known in film. Court records and watermaster reports are the
  primary sources.
- **Source:** Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990), ch. 4; her PhD
  thesis, *Public Entrepreneurship: A Case Study in Ground Water Basin
  Management* (UCLA, 1965); William Blomquist, *Dividing the Waters* (1992).

### Acequias of northern New Mexico

- **What happened:** Community irrigation ditches in the upper Rio Grande,
  brought from Spain via Mexico and operating from the 17th century. Each ditch
  is governed by a *mayordomo* and elected commissioners; water rights come with
  an obligation to turn out for the annual spring cleaning of the ditch. New
  Mexico law recognises acequias as political subdivisions of the state.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The mechanism is **an annual obligatory
  work day that doubles as a membership test**. Turning out for the *limpia* is
  the visible act that keeps a right alive; missing it without sending a
  substitute costs water. The legal recognition is also interesting — a
  customary commons that got itself written into state law rather than being
  displaced by it.
- **Could serve:** *Ostrom and the commons* (S3); *Polycentricity* (S4).
- **Media:** Documented photographically; New Mexico Acequia Association holds
  material. Specific films not verified.
- **Source:** José A. Rivera, *Acequia Culture: Water, Land and Community in the
  Southwest* (University of New Mexico Press, 1998).

## Trade, credit and mutual aid

### The Maghribi traders' coalition

- **What happened:** Jewish traders operating across the 11th-century
  Mediterranean, documented in the Cairo Geniza letters, used overseas agents to
  handle goods they could not accompany. Avner Greif argued they sustained honest
  agency through a coalition in which information about cheating circulated by
  letter and a cheat was refused employment by every member thereafter.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The mechanism on Greif's account is
  **multilateral reputation** — not "I will stop dealing with you", which is
  often too weak because the cheat can find another principal, but "everyone will
  stop dealing with you", which requires a communication network to carry the
  news. That is the cleanest historical illustration of why observability at a
  distance is the binding constraint on trade among strangers.
- **Contested:** Sharply. Sheilagh Ogilvie and Jeremy Edwards (*Economic History
  Review*, 2012) argue no Geniza document actually evidences a coalition, and
  that the Maghribis used courts and bilateral reputation much as European
  traders did. Greif replied in the same issue. Teach the mechanism as a model
  and flag that its best-known historical instance is disputed.
- **Could serve:** *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem* (S3); *Henrich and
  the trust of strangers* (S5).
- **Media:** The Cairo Geniza documents are digitised and viewable (Princeton
  Geniza Project, Cambridge Digital Library) — usable as primary-source imagery.
- **Source:** Avner Greif, "Contract Enforceability and Economic Institutions in
  Early Trade", *AER* 83 (1993); Edwards and Ogilvie, "Contract enforcement,
  institutions and social capital: the Maghribi traders reappraised", *EcHR* 65
  (2012); Greif, "The Maghribi traders: a reappraisal?", same issue.

### The Champagne fairs and the law merchant

- **What happened:** Fairs in Champagne were a hub of long-distance European
  trade in the 12th and 13th centuries, with private judges hearing merchant
  disputes. Milgrom, North and Weingast modelled the private judge as a registry:
  it did not enforce judgments, it recorded who had defaulted, and merchants
  could query it cheaply before dealing.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The mechanism is **a cheap information
  institution substituting for an enforcement institution**. Reputation only
  punishes defection if news travels; the law merchant's contribution is that it
  made the news cheap to obtain, which is a different and much smaller job than
  policing.
- **Contested:** Edwards and Ogilvie have argued the historical evidence for the
  private law merchant is thin and that the fairs relied on the Counts of
  Champagne's enforcement — the same dispute as the Maghribi case, same
  participants.
- **Could serve:** *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem* (S3); *Changing
  the payoffs* (S4).
- **Media:** None known.
- **Source:** Paul Milgrom, Douglass North and Barry Weingast, "The Role of
  Institutions in the Revival of Trade", *Economics and Politics* 2 (1990);
  Sheilagh Ogilvie, *Institutions and European Trade* (Cambridge UP, 2011).

### Medieval and early modern craft guilds

- **What happened:** Guilds regulated training, quality and entry in European
  crafts from roughly the 12th century into the 18th, supplying apprenticeship,
  quality standards, mutual insurance for members, and collective
  representation.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Worth including chiefly for the
  **selective incentives** point — Olson's argument in the flesh. A guild
  supplies a public good (training, standards) and finances it by bundling in
  private benefits only members get: the right to trade in town, sickness and
  burial funds, feast days. But the same structure is also the most-cited case
  for cartel behaviour.
- **Contested:** Ogilvie's work argues the rent-seeking dominated the public
  goods, against an older literature treating guilds as efficient. If the course
  uses guilds as a success, that reading has to be defended, not assumed.
- **Could serve:** *Olson* (S3) — selective incentives; *The bill* (S5), since
  guild goods were funded by excluding outsiders.
- **Media:** Guild halls, ordinances and charters survive widely and are
  photographable.
- **Source:** Sheilagh Ogilvie, *The European Guilds: An Economic Analysis*
  (Princeton UP, 2019); S. R. Epstein, "Craft Guilds, Apprenticeship, and
  Technological Change", *Journal of Economic History* 58 (1998), for the
  opposing view.

### British friendly societies

- **What happened:** Mutual insurance clubs providing sickness pay, burial money
  and sociability to working people, growing through the 19th century to roughly
  six million members by 1904 — on the order of half of all adult males. The
  large federated orders, the Oddfellows and Foresters, each exceeded half a
  million members by 1900. Registration under the Friendly Societies Act 1793
  gave them legal standing.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The central problem is adverse selection
  and malingering — you cannot easily verify that a claimant is too ill to work.
  The mechanism is **local lodges combined with ritual**. Claims were assessed
  by men from the same street and trade who knew whether you were ill, and
  sick-visiting was an obligation. The initiation rituals and regalia, which look
  ornamental, functioned as a barrier and a bond: costly to go through,
  meaningless outside the lodge.
- **Could serve:** *Olson* (S3); *Costly signals and Big Gods* (S5) — the ritual
  point transfers directly; *What modernity already did* (S6), as the
  pre-welfare-state layer the state later absorbed.
- **Media:** Rich material culture — regalia, banners, lodge certificates — well
  represented in UK museum collections. Photographs plentiful.
- **Source:** Simon Cordery, *British Friendly Societies, 1750–1914* (Palgrave,
  2003); P. H. J. H. Gosden, *The Friendly Societies in England, 1815–1875*
  (Manchester UP, 1961).

### Raiffeisen rural credit cooperatives

- **What happened:** Friedrich Wilhelm Raiffeisen, a mayor in the Westerwald,
  organised rural credit associations from the 1860s — the Heddesdorf society of
  1864 is the usual marker — to lend to farmers no commercial bank would touch.
  Members were jointly and severally liable, without limit, for the society's
  debts. The model spread across Germany and then internationally; it is the
  ancestor of much of European cooperative banking.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A credit market failing for lack of
  information about who is a good risk. The mechanism is **unlimited joint
  liability, which converts the lender's monitoring problem into the members'
  own problem**. If your neighbour's default can take your farm, you have both
  the incentive and the local knowledge to screen him — and you had it all along;
  the liability rule is what makes you use it. Membership confined to one parish
  kept the knowledge real.
- **Could serve:** *Changing the payoffs* (S4); *Repeated interaction and the
  folk theorem* (S3).
- **Media:** Raiffeisen museum at Hamm (Sieg), Germany; primary writings
  available. No documentary verified.
- **Source:** Timothy W. Guinnane, "Cooperatives as Information Machines: German
  Rural Credit Cooperatives, 1883–1914", *Journal of Economic History* 61 (2001).

### The Rochdale Pioneers

- **What happened:** Twenty-eight weavers and others opened a cooperative store
  on Toad Lane, Rochdale, in December 1844, on a set of written rules — open
  membership, one member one vote, limited return on capital, and the dividend on
  purchases. The rules were copied widely and became the basis of the
  international cooperative movement.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The mechanism is the **dividend on
  purchases** — a selective incentive that ties a member's private return to
  their own loyalty to the shop, which is exactly the margin on which a consumer
  cooperative dies. One member one vote independent of shareholding is the other
  half: it blocks the takeover that would otherwise convert the thing into an
  ordinary firm.
- **Could serve:** *Olson* (S3); *Changing the payoffs* (S4).
- **Media:** The Toad Lane store is a museum (Rochdale Pioneers Museum); the
  original rules survive.
- **Source:** George Jacob Holyoake, *Self-Help by the People: The History of the
  Rochdale Pioneers* (1858) — contemporary but partisan; Johnston Birchall, *The
  International Co-operative Movement* (Manchester UP, 1997).

### Rotating savings and credit associations

- **What happened:** A group contributes a fixed sum at fixed intervals and the
  whole pot goes to one member each round, until everyone has taken a turn. Found
  independently across the world — *tanda* in Mexico, *susu* in West Africa and
  the Caribbean, *hui* in China, *ko* and *mujin* in Japan, *chit funds* in
  India. Shirley Ardener's 1964 comparative survey is the standard starting
  point.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The structural problem is that whoever
  receives the first pot has every reason to walk away. The mechanism is
  **repetition plus social collateral plus ordering**: the group is drawn from
  people who cannot leave each other's lives — kin, neighbours, colleagues — so
  defection costs the relationship, and in many versions the order of payout is
  itself used as a sanction, with the untrusted placed last.
- **Could serve:** *Introduction to collective action* (S1) — small, vivid,
  immediately graspable; *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem* (S3).
- **Media:** None known in film. Ethnographic photographs exist.
- **Source:** Shirley Ardener, "The Comparative Study of Rotating Credit
  Associations", *Journal of the Royal Anthropological Institute* 94 (1964);
  Clifford Geertz, "The Rotating Credit Association: A 'Middle Rung' in
  Development", *Economic Development and Cultural Change* 10 (1962).

### The Hanseatic League

- **What happened:** A confederation of northern German and Baltic merchant
  towns, operating from roughly the 13th to the 17th century, with trading posts
  (*Kontore*) at Bruges, London, Bergen and Novgorod. It had no standing army, no
  permanent treasury and only an irregular assembly, the *Hansetag*, yet it set
  trading terms, and enforced them against territorial rulers by boycott —
  notably against Bruges and against Norway.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A cooperating body with almost none of
  the apparatus of a state. The mechanism is **collective boycott, backed by
  expulsion**: a town that broke ranks lost Hansa privileges, which was worse
  than whatever it gained. That is a two-level punishment structure — the League
  punishes outsiders, and punishes members who fail to punish.
- **Contested:** The League's cohesion is easy to overstate; it was frequently
  divided, and membership was never crisply defined.
- **Could serve:** *Polycentricity* (S4); *Did it ever work?* (S4).
- **Media:** European Hansemuseum, Lübeck. Charters and Kontor records survive.
- **Source:** Philippe Dollinger, *The German Hansa* (Macmillan, 1970).

### Mondragón

- **What happened:** A federation of worker cooperatives in the Basque Country,
  begun by the priest José María Arizmendiarrieta, with the first cooperative
  (ULGOR) founded in 1956 and the cooperative bank, Caja Laboral, in 1959. It
  grew into one of the largest cooperative groups in the world, spanning
  industry, retail, finance and a university.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Worker cooperatives usually fail to
  scale because they cannot raise capital and cannot discipline shirking. The
  mechanism here is **internal capital plus federation**: the group's own bank
  recycles surpluses into new cooperatives, and inter-cooperative agreements
  pool risk, so a failing cooperative's members are relocated rather than fired.
  Arizmendiarrieta also built the technical school first and the firms second,
  which is a deliberate answer to the selection problem.
- **Contested:** The 2013 collapse of Fagor Electrodomésticos, and the use of
  non-member employees in overseas subsidiaries, are standard criticisms and
  should be stated if the case is used.
- **Could serve:** *Polycentricity* (S4); *Changing the payoffs* (S4).
- **Media:** Several documentaries exist; I have not verified titles well enough
  to name one.
- **Source:** William Foote Whyte and Kathleen King Whyte, *Making Mondragón*
  (ILR Press, 1988); Race Mathews, *Jobs of Our Own* (1999).

## Treaties and agreements

### The Montreal Protocol

- **What happened:** Signed in 1987, in force 1989, phasing out CFCs and other
  ozone-depleting substances. Preceded by the Vienna Convention of 1985 and
  strengthened repeatedly afterwards. It is the standard counter-example to "big
  environmental treaties never work".
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The mechanism worth teaching is not
  goodwill but **a small number of producers, an available substitute, and trade
  restrictions against non-parties**. CFC production was concentrated in a
  handful of firms in a handful of countries, which collapses the *n* in the
  diagnostic; substitutes existed, so the cost of compliance was bounded; and
  the treaty banned trade in controlled substances with non-parties, which makes
  staying outside worse than joining. There was also a fund to pay developing
  countries to comply, which is the selective incentive.
- **Could serve:** *Did it ever work?* (S4) — this is the headline case, run
  against Kyoto; *Introduction to collective action* (S1).
- **Media:** NASA's ozone hole visualisations are strong and public domain. Treaty
  text and negotiating history published by UNEP.
- **Source:** Richard Elliot Benedick, *Ozone Diplomacy* (Harvard UP, 1991) —
  written by the lead US negotiator, so partisan but primary; Scott Barrett,
  *Environment and Statecraft* (Oxford UP, 2003) for the mechanism analysis.

### The Antarctic Treaty

- **What happened:** Signed in Washington in December 1959 by 12 states,
  including the US and USSR, in force 1961. It suspends territorial claims rather
  than resolving them, bans military activity and nuclear tests on the continent,
  provides for freedom of scientific research, and — unusually for its date —
  gives every party the right to inspect any other party's stations at any time,
  without notice.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The mechanism is **unrestricted mutual
  inspection, agreed at the height of the Cold War**, plus the decision to freeze
  rather than settle the underlying dispute. It is a working demonstration of
  Schelling's point that agreement between adversaries turns on verification, not
  trust, and of the idea that an unresolved conflict can be parked if the parking
  is mutual.
- **Could serve:** *Schelling* (S4); *Did it ever work?* (S4); *The first
  enlargement with no outside* (S6), as a partial precedent for governing
  something nobody owns.
- **Media:** Treaty text is short and readable — good primary source for a
  session. IGY-era photographs are plentiful.
- **Source:** The Antarctic Treaty (1959), full text via the Antarctic Treaty
  Secretariat; Barrett, *Environment and Statecraft* (2003).

### Smallpox eradication

- **What happened:** Viktor Zhdanov, Soviet deputy health minister, proposed
  global eradication at the World Health Assembly in 1958, and a programme was
  adopted in 1959. It stalled, and was relaunched as the Intensified Smallpox
  Eradication Programme in 1967, with US funding and CDC staff alongside more
  than a billion doses of Soviet-supplied vaccine. The last naturally occurring
  case was in Somalia in 1977; eradication was certified in 1979 and declared by
  the World Health Assembly in 1980.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A global public good delivered while the
  two funders were pointing missiles at each other. Two mechanisms worth
  separating. First, **the good is indivisible and final** — eradication, once
  achieved, cannot be defected away from, so the payoff structure is a stag hunt
  rather than a dilemma. Second, the operational shift from mass vaccination to
  **surveillance and ring containment** — find a case, vaccinate everyone around
  it — which made the task affordable, and which is an observability fix, not a
  resource fix.
- **Could serve:** *Did it ever work?* (S4); *The games* (S2) as a stag hunt;
  *What modernity already did* (S6).
- **Media:** WHO holds extensive film and photography from the campaign. D. A.
  Henderson's and William Foege's memoirs are primary accounts.
- **Source:** F. Fenner et al., *Smallpox and Its Eradication* (WHO, 1988) — the
  official history, freely available; William H. Foege, *House on Fire* (2011);
  Erez Manela, "A Pox on Your Narrative", *Diplomatic History* 34 (2010).

### The Limited Test Ban Treaty

- **What happened:** Signed in Moscow in August 1963 by the US, USSR and UK,
  banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and underwater, but not
  underground. Negotiated within a year of the Cuban missile crisis, after years
  of failed comprehensive test ban talks.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The instructive part is the failure that
  preceded it. A comprehensive ban was blocked on verification: underground tests
  could not then be distinguished from earthquakes without on-site inspection,
  and neither side would accept inspectors. The parties therefore **agreed only
  over the range where cheating was detectable unilaterally** — atmospheric tests
  show up as fallout, from your own territory, with no inspection required. The
  scope of the agreement was set by the monitoring technology, not by the level
  of trust.
- **Could serve:** *Schelling* (S4) — arguably the single best illustration in
  the course of verification determining what can be agreed; *Did it ever work?*
  (S4).
- **Media:** Extensive footage of atmospheric tests, and of the signing. Kennedy's
  July 1963 address on the treaty is on film.
- **Source:** Glenn T. Seaborg, *Kennedy, Khrushchev and the Test Ban*
  (University of California Press, 1981); Thomas Schelling, *The Strategy of
  Conflict* (1960) and *Arms and Influence* (1966) for the framework.

### Cleaning up the Rhine

- **What happened:** The Rhine ran through several countries with each dumping
  upstream of the next. The International Commission for the Protection of the
  Rhine was formed in 1950 with limited effect. On 1 November 1986 a fire at the
  Sandoz warehouse near Basel washed roughly 20 tonnes of agrochemicals into the
  river, killing fish along hundreds of kilometres. Within a year the states
  adopted the Rhine Action Programme (1987), with salmon returning to the river
  as its public target.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A textbook upstream–downstream
  externality where the upstream party has no incentive to act. The mechanism is
  **a focal disaster plus a simple, publicly legible target**. Nothing about the
  incentives changed in 1986; what changed was that a visible event made
  agreement politically possible and "bring the salmon back" gave a
  non-specialist public something to check progress against. Worth pairing with
  Schelling on focal points.
- **Could serve:** *Did it ever work?* (S4); *Schelling* (S4).
- **Media:** News footage of the Sandoz fire and the fish kill exists (November
  1986). ICPR publishes monitoring data.
- **Source:** Mark A. Cioc, *The Rhine: An Eco-Biography, 1815–2000* (University
  of Washington Press, 2002); ICPR programme documents.

## Cooperative labour

### Barn raising and the Amish frolic

- **What happened:** In North American rural communities, particularly Amish and
  Mennonite ones, the frame of a barn is raised in a single day by scores of
  neighbours who arrive unpaid, with women provisioning and older men directing.
  The practice continues; it is regularly used after fires. It is one instance of
  a broader class of *frolics* — communal work days for threshing, quilting,
  butchering.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A lumpy task nobody can do alone and
  nobody can be paid to do, in a community that rejects insurance. The mechanism
  is **a generalised obligation with no ledger**: nobody records who owes whom,
  which works precisely because the group is closed, small, unlikely to move
  away, and religiously bound. It is an immediately legible demonstration of the
  diagnostic's questions 2 and 4 — everyone can see who turned up, and everyone
  will be there next time.
- **Could serve:** *Introduction to collective action* (S1) — one of the best
  opening images available, and a success rather than a disaster; *The cooperative
  ape* (S3); *Costly signals and Big Gods* (S5).
- **Media:** Photographed constantly; the barn raising sequence in *Witness*
  (Peter Weir, 1985) is the widely known film version, though it is a dramatised
  Hollywood depiction and should be labelled as such. Documentary footage also
  exists.
- **Source:** Donald B. Kraybill, *The Riddle of Amish Culture*, rev. ed. (Johns
  Hopkins UP, 2001); John A. Hostetler, *Amish Society*, 4th ed. (1993).

### Reciprocal labour institutions: ayni, minka, gotong royong, meitheal

- **What happened:** Named institutions of reciprocal labour recur worldwide:
  *ayni* (balanced reciprocal labour between households) and *minka*/*minga*
  (community work for a shared good) in the Andes; *gotong royong* in Indonesia;
  *meitheal* in Ireland; *tequio* and *faena* in Mexico. Several are still
  functioning, and some are written into law — Mexican municipalities in Oaxaca
  govern partly through *usos y costumbres* including tequio obligations.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Useful for a specific analytical point:
  **the difference between the balanced and the pooled forms**. *Ayni* is a
  bilateral debt — you worked my field, I owe you a day — and is enforced by
  memory of an account between two households. *Minka* is a contribution to a
  shared good with no individual counterparty, and is enforced by the community
  as a whole with sanctions on non-attendance. That is the two-player case and
  the *n*-player case, named separately by the people who use them.
- **Contested:** Terminology and practice vary considerably between regions and
  the literature does not use the words consistently; check any specific claim
  against a regional ethnography rather than a general source.
- **Could serve:** *The cooperative ape* (S3); *Olson* (S3), on the difference
  size makes.
- **Media:** Ethnographic photography exists. No specific film verified.
- **Source:** For the Andes, Enrique Mayer, *The Articulated Peasant* (Westview,
  2002), and Catherine J. Allen, *The Hold Life Has* (Smithsonian, 1988).

## Cooperation under war

### The live and let live system in the trenches

- **What happened:** Along stretches of the Western Front between 1914 and 1918,
  opposing units arrived at tacit truces: shelling at predictable times and
  places, deliberate inaccuracy, ration parties left alone, no firing during
  bad weather. Tony Ashworth documented the practice from diaries, letters and
  veterans' testimony, and showed it was widespread rather than exceptional. High
  commands attacked it with raiding orders, which was how it was eventually
  broken.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The best available natural experiment on
  repeated play. The mechanism is **an iterated prisoner's dilemma with
  reciprocity and a long shadow of the future** — units facing each other for
  weeks, able to observe and retaliate immediately, with no communication
  required. What destroyed it is as instructive as what created it: the
  high command rotated units and ordered raids, which is a deliberate removal of
  repetition by a third party who wanted defection.
- **Could serve:** *Repeated interaction and the folk theorem* (S3); *The
  evolution of cooperation* (S3); *War* (S1).
- **Media:** Abundant WWI photography and film at the Imperial War Museum.
  Ashworth's source diaries are archival.
- **Source:** Tony Ashworth, *Trench Warfare 1914–1918: The Live and Let Live
  System* (Macmillan, 1980); Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation*
  (1984), ch. 4, which reads Ashworth through tit-for-tat.

### The Christmas truce, 1914

- **What happened:** Over Christmas 1914, unofficial truces broke out at many
  points along the Western Front — soldiers met in no man's land, exchanged
  items, buried their dead, and in some places played football. It was widely
  reported in the press at the time and suppressed in subsequent years.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Worth teaching as a **focal point**
  rather than as a warm story. The problem for two hostile lines is that stepping
  out first is fatal unless you know the other side will hold fire, and there is
  no way to negotiate. A date both sides recognise as sacred solves the
  coordination problem without communication — it is common knowledge, and that
  is all that is required. The truce's fragility is also the point: it lasted
  precisely as long as the focal date did.
- **Contested:** Romanticised heavily, including the football, whose extent is
  disputed by historians. Say so.
- **Media:** Contemporary photographs exist. *Joyeux Noël* (Christian Carion,
  2005) is the feature film; the Sainsbury's 2014 advertisement is a
  commercial dramatisation and best avoided. Letters home are the primary source
  and are published.
- **Source:** Malcolm Brown and Shirley Seaton, *Christmas Truce* (Hodder, 1984);
  Stanley Weintraub, *Silent Night* (2001).

### The Danish rescue, October 1943

- **What happened:** When the German occupation ordered the deportation of
  Denmark's Jews in early October 1943, warning circulated in advance and roughly
  7,200 Jews and some 700 non-Jewish relatives — around 95% of the community —
  were hidden and ferried across the Øresund to neutral Sweden by fishermen over
  about three weeks. 482 were caught and deported to Theresienstadt, where most
  survived, partly because Danish officials kept demanding access to them.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A very large number of strangers had to
  act, each at real risk, with no organisation, no leader and no time. The
  mechanism is worth being honest about rather than moralising: Denmark's
  occupation was unusually light and the Danish administration remained in place,
  which lowered the individual cost; the operation was partly paid for, with
  fishermen charging substantial fares; the sea crossing was short; and Sweden
  publicly announced it would accept the refugees, which supplied the destination
  and the common knowledge. Cooperation at that scale and speed needed the risk
  to be survivable and the destination to be certain.
- **Contested:** The account is often told as spontaneous national virtue. The
  fares charged, the earlier Danish cooperation with the occupation, and Sweden's
  role complicate it. Bo Lidegaard's account addresses these.
- **Could serve:** *Introduction to collective action* (S1); *The cooperative
  ape* (S3); *War* (S1).
- **Media:** USHMM and Yad Vashem hold photographs and survivor testimony. The
  boats survive. *Miracle at Midnight* (1998) is a Disney TV dramatisation —
  check before using.
- **Source:** Bo Lidegaard, *Countrymen* (Knopf, 2013); Leni Yahil, *The Rescue of
  Danish Jewry* (JPS, 1969); USHMM Holocaust Encyclopedia, "Rescue in Denmark".

### Le Chambon-sur-Lignon

- **What happened:** A Protestant village and the surrounding plateau in
  south-central France sheltered several thousand refugees, many of them Jewish
  children, between 1940 and 1944, organised in part around the pastor André
  Trocmé and the local Huguenot congregations. The figures usually given are in
  the range of 3,000–5,000 and are not precisely established.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The interesting question is why
  concealment held when one informer could have ended it. The mechanism appears
  to be **a pre-existing minority identity with its own memory of persecution,
  and a network of congregations with a habit of collective action** — the
  Huguenot history is doing work here that a generic appeal to conscience does
  not explain. Also an instance of deliberate non-observability: people were not
  told details they did not need, which limits what any single defection could
  destroy.
- **Contested:** How centrally Trocmé organised things, versus a diffuse plateau
  effort, is disputed; Hallie's account is the heroic version and later
  historians have qualified it.
- **Could serve:** *The cooperative ape* (S3); *Costly signals and Big Gods*
  (S5); *Introduction to collective action* (S1).
- **Media:** *Weapons of the Spirit* (Pierre Sauvage, 1989) — documentary, made
  by someone born there while hidden. Strong primary material.
- **Source:** Philip Hallie, *Lest Innocent Blood Be Shed* (Harper & Row, 1979);
  Caroline Moorehead, *Village of Secrets* (2014), which challenges parts of it.

## Standards and infrastructure

### The Metre Convention

- **What happened:** Signed in Paris on 20 May 1875 by 17 states, creating the
  International Bureau of Weights and Measures and a permanent structure for
  maintaining physical standards. Prototype metre and kilogram artefacts were
  manufactured and distributed to signatories in 1889. The structure it created
  still maintains the SI.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A pure coordination problem — the payoff
  comes from everyone using the same unit and nobody much cares which one, except
  that incumbents have sunk costs in their own. The mechanism is **a permanent
  neutral body holding the physical artefact**: rather than states agreeing on a
  definition and each interpreting it, one institution keeps the object and
  everyone else calibrates against it. The tell is that Britain and the US signed
  and still did not adopt metric domestically for a century, which shows how
  weak the mechanism is without a reason to actually switch.
- **Could serve:** *The games* (S2), as a coordination problem distinct from a
  dilemma; *What modernity already did* (S6).
- **Media:** The prototype artefacts are photographed and displayed; BIPM
  publishes historical material.
- **Source:** The Metre Convention (1875), text via BIPM; Ken Alder, *The Measure
  of All Things* (Free Press, 2002), for the earlier French history.

### Standard time and the prime meridian

- **What happened:** North American railways adopted standardised time zones on
  18 November 1883, replacing a patchwork of local solar times that made
  timetables and safe scheduling nearly impossible. The International Meridian
  Conference in Washington in October 1884 adopted Greenwich as the prime
  meridian, with France abstaining and continuing to use the Paris meridian for
  decades.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** Coordination again, with a visible
  distributional fight over *which* convention, which is the part usually left
  out. The mechanism is **a private actor large enough to move first**: the
  railways imposed the zones on themselves before any government legislated, and
  everyone else followed because clocks that disagreed with the station clock
  were useless. That is Olson's privileged group — one player big enough to
  supply the good alone — and it is rare enough to be worth a case.
- **Could serve:** *Olson* (S3) — the privileged group; *The games* (S2); *Why
  this matters so much now* (S1), on infrastructure preceding governance.
- **Media:** The 1884 conference proceedings are published and readable.
  Photographs of railway time standardisation exist.
- **Source:** Ian R. Bartky, *Selling the True Time* (Stanford UP, 2000);
  proceedings of the International Meridian Conference (1884).

### The International Telegraph Union and the Universal Postal Union

- **What happened:** The International Telegraph Union was founded in Paris in
  1865 by 20 states to make telegrams cross borders without being re-transcribed
  at each frontier. The General Postal Union was founded at Bern in 1874 and
  renamed the Universal Postal Union in 1878, on the principle that each country
  keeps the postage it collects and delivers incoming foreign mail without
  further charge. Both still exist, now as UN agencies.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The UPU's mechanism is the elegant one.
  Settling accounts between every pair of countries for every letter is
  administratively impossible, so the UPU **abolished the accounting instead of
  automating it** — you keep what you collect, you deliver what arrives, and it
  roughly balances. An agreement that removes the need for monitoring is
  structurally stronger than one that monitors well, and that generalises.
- **Could serve:** *Changing the payoffs* (S4); *What modernity already did*
  (S6); *Polycentricity* (S4).
- **Media:** UPU monument in Bern; founding treaty texts published. No film
  verified.
- **Source:** Treaty of Bern (1874), text via UPU; Craig N. Murphy,
  *International Organization and Industrial Change* (Polity, 1994).

### Container shipping standards

- **What happened:** Malcom McLean ran the first container voyage in 1956.
  Incompatible box sizes and corner fittings across carriers then threatened to
  make the technology useless, since a container that cannot be lifted by the
  next port's crane is just a badly shaped crate. Standard dimensions and corner
  castings were settled through US and ISO committee work in the early and
  mid-1960s, with ISO standards issued from 1968. Costs collapsed and world trade
  patterns changed.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A network good whose value to each
  adopter depends on how many others adopted, with incumbents holding
  incompatible proprietary designs. The mechanism is **standardisation imposed
  partly by a large buyer** — US military shipping requirements during Vietnam
  pushed the industry toward common dimensions faster than negotiation alone
  would have. Another privileged-group case, and a reminder that standards bodies
  often ratify what a big player has already forced.
- **Could serve:** *Olson* (S3); *Changing the payoffs* (S4); *Why this matters
  so much now* (S1), on the speed of technology relative to governance.
- **Media:** Extensive photography of early container operations. Levinson's book
  is the accessible account.
- **Source:** Marc Levinson, *The Box: How the Shipping Container Made the World
  Smaller and the World Economy Bigger* (Princeton UP, 2006).

## Knowledge commons before the internet

### The Republic of Letters

- **What happened:** From the 16th to the 18th century, European scholars
  maintained a correspondence network across religious and political lines,
  exchanging results, specimens, books and introductions. Some correspondents —
  Mersenne in Paris, Oldenburg in London, Peiresc in Provence — acted as hubs,
  relaying letters between people who had never met.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** A positive commons where the failure
  mode is undersupply — the individual incentive is to keep a discovery private
  until you can profit from it, which is what the alchemical tradition did. The
  mechanism is **credit: contribution converted into reputation, and reputation
  into position**. Sharing became the way to be known, and being known was the
  way to get patronage, so disclosure and self-interest were aligned rather than
  opposed. The hubs matter too — a network with brokers reaches far more people
  per letter written.
- **Could serve:** *Ostrom and the commons* (S3), under positive commons;
  *Henrich and the trust of strangers* (S5).
- **Media:** The Stanford *Mapping the Republic of Letters* project has
  visualisations of the correspondence networks, and much of the correspondence
  is digitised and online (Early Modern Letters Online, Oxford).
- **Source:** Anne Goldgar, *Impolite Learning* (Yale UP, 1995); Paula Findlen,
  *Possessing Nature* (1994).

### Philosophical Transactions and the invention of priority

- **What happened:** The Royal Society's *Philosophical Transactions*, begun by
  Henry Oldenburg in 1665, published dated accounts of experimental findings and
  correspondence. Before it, discoveries were routinely concealed or published as
  anagrams to reserve a claim without revealing it. Dated publication gave a
  public record of who had found what, and when.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** This is the mechanism entry for positive
  commons and it deserves to be taught separately from the Republic of Letters.
  **Priority is a selective incentive built on top of a public good.** Publishing
  gives the finding away, which is the contribution; the date stamp gives the
  author something nobody else can have, which is the payment. It converts a good
  that is undersupplied into one people compete to supply. Nearly everything
  about modern open knowledge is downstream of it.
- **Could serve:** *Olson* (S3) — selective incentives, in a form the audience
  won't expect; *Ostrom and the commons* (S3), positive commons; *The reframe*
  (S2).
- **Media:** The full run of *Philosophical Transactions* is digitised and openly
  accessible via the Royal Society. Early issues make good slide material.
- **Source:** Robert K. Merton, "Priorities in Scientific Discovery", *American
  Sociological Review* 22 (1957); Mario Biagioli and Peter Galison (eds),
  *Scientific Authorship* (2003); Royal Society archives.

### The International Geophysical Year, 1957–58

- **What happened:** An 18-month coordinated global research programme running
  July 1957 to December 1958, involving around 67 countries including both the US
  and the USSR, covering the upper atmosphere, oceans, glaciology and Antarctica.
  Data were deposited in World Data Centres deliberately duplicated across
  political blocs so that no party could withhold them. Sputnik was launched as
  an IGY contribution. The Antarctic Treaty came directly out of it.
- **Why it's a collective action case:** The mechanism is **mirrored archives**:
  rather than trusting the other side to share, each bloc held a full copy of the
  data, which removed the incentive to withhold by removing the advantage of
  withholding. That is a structural solution to an information commons problem
  during a period of maximum distrust, and it is a design move rather than a
  gesture of goodwill.
- **Could serve:** *Ostrom and the commons* (S3), positive commons; *Schelling*
  (S4); *Did it ever work?* (S4).
- **Media:** Substantial contemporary film and photography; NOAA and NAS hold
  IGY records and the World Data Centre system still exists in successor form.
- **Source:** Fae L. Korsmo, "The Origins and Principles of the World Data Center
  System", *Data Science Journal* (2010); Walter Sullivan, *Assault on the
  Unknown* (McGraw-Hill, 1961) — contemporary journalism, so check specifics.

## Gaps worth filling

Things this list does not yet cover, noted so the next pass doesn't have to
rediscover them:

- Islamic *waqf* endowments — a long-lived institution for funding public goods,
  running for centuries. Included here only as a pointer because I have not
  checked the literature.
- Indigenous fire management regimes in Australia and North America, which are
  commons institutions of a kind the Ostrom literature largely misses.
- The abolition of the Atlantic slave trade as a case of a state acting against
  its own short-run interest and then coercing others into the same position —
  a coercion case, and an ugly one, since enforcement was done by the Royal Navy.
- Swiss *Allmend* beyond Törbel, and the Alpine commons of Austria and Italy,
  where the comparative record is better than the single-village accounts
  suggest.
- The Grameen Bank group lending model (1976 onward), which is joint liability
  again and sits just inside the pre-1990 boundary.
