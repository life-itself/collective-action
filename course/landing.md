# Stag & Moloch: Intro to Collective Action

Three environmental activists run a clandestine sabotage campaign for the better part of a decade. It takes extraordinary trust – no names, no records, no way to check up on anyone. Then they get arrested, and within weeks they're informing on each other.

That's [*If a Tree Falls*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qwQoi0DoOKM), and it's where we start.

You can draw the situation those three were in on the back of an envelope: a 2x2, four boxes, one bad equilibrium. It is the same 2x2 you draw for five AI labs racing toward something none of them wants. Six orders of magnitude apart, structurally identical, and once you can see it you cannot stop seeing it.

## Why bother if you already buy the diagnosis?

If you're reading this you probably already think collective action failure sits underneath the [metacrisis](https://metacrisis.info). So do I. It sits underneath climate change. It sits underneath the AI arms race, nuclear weapons, bioweapons – in each case almost every one of us might want to stop, or go slower, and we can't. And it sits underneath things we don't usually file in the same drawer: nihilism, anomie, loneliness. It makes a certain amount of sense for me to sit in my room and watch Netflix, given what everyone else is doing.

There is a collective action problem of *us*.

I'm not going to spend six sessions establishing that. Our scene is good at the diagnosis and much weaker on the machinery, and the machinery is a hundred years of serious work – Olson, Schelling, Hardin, Ostrom, Axelrod, Henrich – that mostly gets waved at rather than used. So: why this happens exactly, which escape routes exist, which ones have been tried at scale, which worked, and what the historical record says about how we ever got from bands of 150 to a planet of 8 billion.

Two warnings about what you're signing up for.

**The first half is game theory**, and I know how that lands with some of this audience – reductive, modernist, homo economicus, exactly the worldview we're supposed to be leaving behind. I'd say the opposite. The most important thing the theory tells us is that defection here is not a deficiency. Nobody in these stories is stupid or wicked or insufficiently developed. They are being sensible, and the outcome is still terrible. Any account that locates the problem in people being unenlightened has misread the situation, and will prescribe accordingly.

**The second is that I intend to argue with half my own title.** Most people reading this know Moloch, and it has done real work in getting the pattern into circulation. I still think it's the wrong frame: it personifies a structure, and turns a design problem into fate. Stag hunt on one side, Moloch on the other – the course lives in the fight between them.

Where it's all going: every jump in the scale of human cooperation – band to village to chiefdom to city to nation – was a cultural innovation. Ritual, kinship, religion, law, money, the firm. Cultural evolution is the answer to collective action at scale, and the last two sessions are about what that means for the jump we're currently failing to make.

It comes with a bill, though, which we'll look at squarely. Every enlargement of the circle so far has been paid for by whoever was left outside it.

## What you'll leave with

A diagnostic you can run on your own problem – five questions about players, observability, punishment, repetition and size that tell you which kind of trouble you're in and which fixes are even available to you. We'll run it on every case in every session, including yours if you bring one.

Working game theory, built from scratch, no prior exposure assumed.

And the long arc: which interventions have actually worked, over ten thousand years and over the last fifty, and why most of the recent ones failed.

## Course outline

### 1. Why do even environmentalists rat on each other when they get arrested?

We open on *If a Tree Falls* – watch it beforehand – and put it straight next to the AI labs, because a small-group betrayal story used to motivate planetary problems teaches people that collective action failure is about individuals being treacherous, and I want that reading dead by the end of the first hour. Then more cases: Horatio at the bridge, cattle turning to face down a predator. Cooperation isn't a human invention and it isn't reliable in humans either.

We close on agriculture, which is the original version of all of this. Hunter-gatherers had a pretty good life. Then we fell into farming, which nobody chose and which made most people worse off for millennia.

### 2. Why don't rational people cooperate?

Prisoner's dilemma, stag hunt, tragedy of the commons, public goods, free riding. Three or four games done properly rather than a tour.

Two things I want to land. Defection is not a deficiency – see above, and it's worth an hour. And the reframe: the literature's framing is unattractive, since even the prisoner's dilemma is a story about betrayal, and it reads better the other way round. A collective action *opportunity*. The question stops being 'we had a good state and fell out of it' and becomes 'the bad state is the default, so how do we ever reach the good one?'

Then the many names – multipolar trap, race to the bottom, social dilemma, coordination failure, Moloch – and my argument with the last of them.

### 3. Are we doomed? Escape routes at village scale

Olson first, because *The Logic of Collective Action* is the baseline everything else improves on: group size, latent groups, selective incentives, and the privileged group where one player is big enough to act alone.

Then repeated interaction and the folk theorem – the shadow of the future, and what it actually requires. Axelrod's tournaments and Nowak's grids, where your neighbors rather than strangers are who you play. Ostrom's design principles as the empirical version of the same list. (Ostrom I know less well than I should, and forcing that reading is one of my own reasons for running this.)

Also a distinction that gets skipped: the commons we can *overuse*, like lobster grounds and alpine pasture, are not the same problem as the commons we have to *create*, like the information commons. Different failure, different fix.

Everything in this session runs on repetition, observation and punishment among people who know each other. At village scale it works. With strangers it breaks.

### 4. What do you do when you can't see who defected?

Hobbes, and Hardin's own answer, which the people who quote him usually forget: mutual coercion, mutually agreed upon. The state is a collective action technology and the most consequential one we've built on purpose. Then changing the payoffs – taxes, subsidies, property rights, Coase – and where markets run out.

Schelling gets real time here. Focal points, credible commitment, cooperating with an adversary you can't trust. He was writing about nuclear arms races and the AI version rhymes uncomfortably closely.

Then nesting, which is the piece people forget: polycentricity, Ostrom's answer to scale. You don't scale a village, you stack villages.

And the applied record, which for most of you is the practical payoff – Montreal against Kyoto, non-proliferation, fisheries. Why a handful worked and most didn't, and what it turned on.

### 5. How did we get from 150 people to 8 billion?

Multi-level selection, and the genetic story of why we can cooperate at all. Then cultural group selection, where cultures that cooperate outcompete cultures that don't, and the coevolution of the two.

Ilahita: ritual, initiation and manufactured kinship holding together a village of thousands that on the standard account should have split long before. Turchin's *Ultrasociety* and war as the ratchet. Sosis and Norenzayan on costly signals and moralizing gods, which is the mechanism by which religion does this work rather than the assertion that it does. Henrich on the Church dissolving kin networks, and the strange new thing that becomes possible afterwards: trusting someone you've never met and won't meet again.

We end on the bill. Cooperation scales by building in-groups, and an in-group needs an out-group – Turchin's ratchet is war, after all. Every enlargement of the circle has been paid for by someone outside it.

### 6. Can the circle close?

Modernity already ran one enlargement: we're all brothers and sisters, there are human rights, there are international institutions. It was real, it's incomplete, and it's stalling. Nation-states and markets are not going to hold AI, climate and the biosphere.

And the jump we need now is unlike any of the previous ones, because every earlier enlargement had an outside – to define itself against, to compete with, to send the bill to. This one has none. No external enemy, nobody to defect against, nobody else to pay. That's the whole difficulty, and it's why more of what worked before won't be enough.

Which leaves the question the course has been walking toward. Each cultural paradigm widens the sphere of what we treat as ours to care about; if we need planetary cooperation we need something that binds us as humanity; and the honest question is whether 'religion' is the word for that.

We'll finish with a love letter to an Earth ending.
